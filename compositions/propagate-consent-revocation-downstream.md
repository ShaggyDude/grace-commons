## Summary

This composition is a regulated composition (a spec that wires two or more atoms — freestanding, self-contained pattern specs — together) that solves a problem none of its constituents solves alone: ensuring that a system processes personal data on a consent basis only while valid consent exists, that the people authorized to record and withdraw consent are themselves authorized to do so, that consent records survive for the regulator-mandated proof period, and — the property that defeats naive implementations — that withdrawing consent *propagates*: the act of revocation records, atomically and tamper-evidently, the complete set of downstream processing activities that were relying on the now-withdrawn consent. It wires four constituents: Consent (the data subject's grant/revoke/expire lifecycle with its point-in-time `check`), Permissions (the inward authorization surface governing which internal actor may administer consent), the Audit Trail substrate (the tamper-evident — designed so unauthorized changes are detectable — regulated-audit substrate that attribution-stamps and seals every state-changing decision, and through which Event Log, Actor Identity, the audit-event Retention Window, and Tamper Evidence are reached transitively), and a consent-record Retention Window instance (the policy-bounded lifetime holding each consent record as demonstrability evidence, distinct from the substrate's audit-event instance).

The composition's defining emergent guarantee (a property that appears only when atoms are combined — no single atom carries it) has two halves. *Consent-gates-processing:* a single read-only query, `processing_permitted(subject_ref, purpose)`, returns `permitted` if and only if Consent reports valid consent (`check` → `granted`) for that subject and purpose at the current time. Processing systems consume this query rather than reading Consent directly, so the gate is implemented exactly once instead of re-implemented — or skipped — per processing system. *Revocation propagates:* [Withdraw Consent] revokes the consent and then records a `consent.revoked` Audit Trail event whose `affected_scopes` set is exactly the downstream processing scopes registered against that consent. These two writes cannot be made one — neither can be taken back once made, so no transaction spans them — so they are **ordered** instead: the revoke first, the propagation event second. That ordering gives the guarantee an auditor needs without qualification, since a propagation record always has a real revocation behind it, and it leaves exactly one gap — a revocation whose propagation record has not yet landed — which is always visible, always repaired within a declared window, and never a processing-suppression gap, because the gate reads the consent store directly and is honest the instant the revoke commits.

Beyond the gate and the propagation, the composition guarantees that every consent grant is attributed and tamper-evidently recorded, that consent records are held under a retention floor that survives revocation (a withdrawn consent is evidence, not garbage — it must outlive its own withdrawal for the regulator's proof period), that the complete consent lifecycle is reconstructable from the records alone, and that every consent-administration action passes a Permissions check before it touches the consent store.

 Its most common uses are advertising and marketing consent management under GDPR (the EU General Data Protection Regulation)/ePrivacy, health-app and research consent under HIPAA (the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) Authorization, cookie-consent backends under the ePrivacy Directive, and any system that must prove, from records alone, that consent-based processing was gated on valid consent and that every withdrawal's downstream impact was enumerated and sealed.

## Intent

Every system that processes personal data on the basis of consent faces the same regulated obligation, and it is the same whether the system is an advertising platform, a health app, an analytics pipeline, or a cookie-banner backend. Before processing for a consent-based purpose, the system must confirm a valid consent exists; the data subject must be able to withdraw that consent as easily as they gave it; and — the limb that defeats most real deployments — when consent is withdrawn, the systems that were relying on it must *stop*, which means the withdrawal cannot be a private fact buried in one consent store but must propagate to every downstream process that was acting on the basis now gone. GDPR Article 6(1)(a) names consent as a lawful basis; Article 7 fixes its conditions (freely given, specific, informed, demonstrable); Article 7(3) requires withdrawal be as easy as grant and, read with Article 17(1)(b), that withdrawal end the basis for future processing. The domain varies; the structural obligation is constant: gate processing on consent, and make withdrawal propagate provably.

No constituent, alone, enforces this arc. Consent owns the grant/revoke/expire lifecycle and the `check` query that answers *is there valid consent for this subject and purpose at this time?* — but it deliberately stops there: its own specification states *"the atom does not enforce processing suppression"* and names downstream propagation as out of scope, handled by *"this composition."* Consent records that consent was withdrawn; it neither knows what downstream processing relied on it nor records which processes must now cease. Permissions owns inward authorization — *which internal actor may grant, revoke, or read a consent record on a data subject's behalf* — but it knows nothing of consent state. The Audit Trail substrate records attributed, tamper-evident, retention-bounded events but does not know which events constitute a consent's propagation history. The consent-record Retention Window instance holds each consent record for its proof period but knows nothing of consent semantics. The structure that makes the four coherent as a single consent-management surface — the gate that centralizes the consent precondition, the downstream-registration index that makes "what relied on this consent" a recorded fact, and the propagation event that fires atomically with revocation — belongs to no single constituent. It belongs to the composition, and this composition is that structure.

This is a composition, not a new primitive. Consent, Permissions, Audit Trail, and Retention Window are unchanged; the composition is the wiring that makes them coherent as a single consent-management surface. It introduces emergent actions — [Record Consent], [Register Processing], [Withdraw Consent], [Processing Permitted] — that belong to no single constituent and exist only because the constituents are wired together. The [Withdraw Consent] action, in particular, wraps a Consent `revoke`, an enumeration of the consent's registered downstream scopes, and the tamper-evident propagation event into one named, atomic surface, so that withdrawing consent is a single auditable act whose downstream impact is recorded — not a Consent `revoke` whose consequences leak silently into whatever processing systems happen to re-check, or fail to re-check, the consent store.

What the composition is *not*: it is not the consent-collection UI (the banner, the form, the verbal-capture integration that produces the `grant` signal — that is upstream, producing the inputs [Record Consent] records); it is not the data-erasure engine (whether withdrawal triggers deletion of derived data under Article 17 is the downstream processor's obligation, signalled by the propagation event, not performed by this composition); it is not a lawful-basis adjudicator for non-consent bases (legitimate interest, contract necessity, legal obligation are GDPR Article 6 bases this composition does not model — a Customer Due Diligence obligation under Customer Onboarding rests on Article 6(1)(c), not consent, and is explicitly out of scope here); and it is not the access-control surface for internal operators beyond the consent-administration actions it gates. Each is named explicitly in Edge cases.

## Composes

- **[Consent](../atoms/consent.md)** — the primary data-bearing constituent: the data subject's grant/revoke/expire lifecycle (Granted → Revoked | Expired) with the point-in-time `check(subject_ref, purpose, at_time?) → granted | revoked | expired | not-known`. The composition calls `grant` (from [Record Consent]), `revoke` (from [Withdraw Consent]), `check` (from the [Processing Permitted] gate), and `read` (from [Read Consent History]). The composition maintains exactly one Consent store instance, and it requires **exclusive write ownership** of it: every `grant` and `revoke` against this instance flows through [Record Consent] and [Withdraw Consent] — a consent granted by a direct atom-level `Consent.grant` call would pass the [Processing Permitted] gate (the gate reads `Consent.check`, which knows the record) while having no registration surface, no retention placement, no audit event, and no path to withdrawal-with-propagation through this composition. Such a direct write is a **composition-bypass finding**, detected from the records on both sides of the ownership claim: a direct `grant` by Generation acceptance check 1's symmetric reading (a Consent store record with no `consent.granted` event), and a direct `revoke` by check 6's case (v) as check 7 and the reconciliation scan apply it (a Revoked record with no `consent.withdrawal_intended` event naming it — a withdrawal that never entered this composition, which no compensation may touch). Read freedom is scoped, not blanket: writes are exclusively owned; `Consent.check` is consumed **via the [Processing Permitted] gate** (a processing system reading `check` directly is Invariant 1(b)'s bypass), and `Consent.read` is consumed **via [Read Consent History]** (a direct `read` returns regulated records with no Permissions gate and no `consent.history-read` meta-event). Keeping direct constituent reads out of the deployment is a named read-side deployment obligation, the read mirror of the exclusive-write obligation. Consent's own specification names this composition by name as the home of the propagation it deliberately excludes (Consent Edge cases — *Consent withdrawal propagation*); this composition is that home.
- **[Permissions](../atoms/permissions.md)** — the inward authorization surface, a composing **peer** of Consent made operational here: Consent governs what the system may do *to the data subject's data* (outward authorization, held by the subject); Permissions governs which internal actor may *administer* a consent record (inward authorization, held by the operator). The composition calls `permitted(actor_ref, scope)` before every consent-administration action, with the scope vocabulary [Consent Grant], [Consent Revoke], [Consent Register Processing], and [Consent Read]. The composition maintains exactly one Permissions instance. The gate query [Processing Permitted] does **not** consult Permissions — it is the data subject's consent that gates processing, not an internal actor's permission; conflating the two is the exact error Consent's Intent warns against, and keeping them distinct is load-bearing (Invariant 7).
- **[Audit Trail](./audit-trail.md)** — the regulated-audit substrate. Every state-changing action the composition exposes records here as one `record_action` call — consumed at the substrate's declared contract, `record_action(action_ref, actor_ref, credential, data) → event_id | rejected(invalid-credential | invalid-request | recording-failure(step))`, the `(step)` payload carried on every transcription (Action wiring) — producing an Event Log entry, an Actor Identity attestation, a Retention Window record (for the audit event), and a Tamper Evidence seal per the substrate's cadence. The composition maintains exactly one Audit Trail instance configured with the host's regulatory retention policy for audit events. **Actor Identity is reached transitively through Audit Trail** — the composition does not maintain a separate Actor Identity instance; the `actor_ref` and `credential` passed to each composition action flow to `AuditTrail.record_action`, which binds the actor cryptographically. Event Log, Tamper Evidence, and the audit-event Retention Window are likewise reached transitively.
- **[Retention Window](../atoms/retention-window.md)** — the policy-bounded record lifetime, used here for **one placement over each consent record** (distinct from the audit-event Retention Window inside the substrate): a *consent-record* placement at [Record Consent] governing how long the controller is **obliged to be able to produce** the consent record (GDPR's "as long as necessary" plus the controller's demonstrability burden under Article 7(1) — a withdrawn consent must be retained as proof the basis once existed and was lawfully ended). The placement states an obligation and its floor; it never ends the record's existence: Consent's Invariant 8 keeps every consent record in the store, and Retention Window's `Purged` is by that atom's own definition the state of a retention whose underlying record *has been destroyed* — so a consent-record retention is never lawfully purged, and the elapse of the obligation is read from Retention Window's `Purge Eligible` projection, not from a transition (Edge cases — *A consent-record retention never reaches `Purged`*). This composition calls `place_under_retention` only and never `purge` on this instance, so Retention Window's purge-family rejections (`not-retained`, `retention-period-not-elapsed`, and that family's kin) do not surface at this composition's boundary. This Retention Window instance is **distinct from the Retention Window instance inside the Audit Trail substrate**, which governs the audit *events* (see Edge cases — *Two Retention Window instances*).

The Event Log, Actor Identity, Tamper Evidence, and audit-event Retention Window atoms are reached transitively through Audit Trail; the composition does not maintain separate instances of those atoms at this layer.

## Composition logic

### Composition state

The composition owns emergent state that wires the constituents into one queryable consent-management surface. None of this state belongs to a single constituent — and none of it holds truth the constituents lack: every element carries its Contract classification per [`execution-contract.md`](../execution-contract.md) §Composition state, and all three are **derived indexes** over the audit events (and constituent stores), each with a named rebuild procedure.

- **`consent_to_downstream`** — map from `consent_id` to the set of registered downstream processing bindings; **element identity is the pair `{processing_scope, processor_ref}`**, each element carrying a `registered_at` attribute fixed at the pair's *first* registration (a re-registration records its own event but neither duplicates the element nor advances its `registered_at`). A binding is added by [Register Processing] **audit-first** — only after its `processing.registered` event lands — so every element of the set traces to an immutable audit event. [Withdraw Consent] reads this set to compute the [Affected Scopes] it records on the propagation event. The set is never silently pruned: once a processing activity is registered against a consent, it remains in the set for the life of the consent record, so the propagation event records the complete history of what the consent governed, not merely what was active at an arbitrary moment. This is the auditor's first query surface for *what must stop when this consent is withdrawn*. **Contract classification: derived index.** *Rebuild procedure:* traverse the Audit Trail for `processing.registered` events carrying this `consent_id` and take `{processing_scope, processor_ref, registered_at}` from each payload — [Register Processing] step 4 writes all three into the event, so the binding facts are immutable audit content and the rebuild is total **for as long as those events retain their payloads — which is a bound, and calling the rebuild total without it was the overstatement this class exists to find**. The substrate destroys an event's payload in its entirety at the audit instance's horizon, leaving the event's `event_id`, `sequence_number` and `recorded_at`, and — through the destruction record's `(event_id, attestation_id)` pair — the attestation's `action_ref`, `actor_ref` and `attested_at`. So a purged `processing.registered` event remains *identifiable*; what is gone is the `{processing_scope, processor_ref, registered_at}` it carried, which lived only in the payload. An auditor can still say a registration happened and who recorded it, and can no longer say what was registered. **No second source holds these bindings** — `processing_scope` and `processor_ref` are a composition-introduced registry, not a constituent's record — so past the horizon the fact is gone rather than merely harder to reach, and this map's entries split by retention state: *derived index* while their registration events retain their payloads, **not rebuildable** beyond. The consequence is bounded in one direction and not the other: the **compensation** path is safe, because it re-derives the set inside the declared `compensation_window` and is nowhere near the horizon; **Generation acceptance check 2** is not, because it verifies a propagation event's `affected_scopes` against the registrations before that withdrawal's intent event, and an auditor runs it whenever they run it. Past the horizon that verification is unavailable — which is a limit of the check, not a defect in the fulfillment, and check 9 says so rather than letting it read as a coverage failure; elements are keyed by the pair, keeping the *earliest* `registered_at` where re-registration events legitimately repeat a pair. The Contract's three obligations follow: the map sits **outside the actions' atomicity surface**; a missing or lost entry is a **rebuild trigger, not data loss** — and because [Withdraw Consent] step 4 reads this index as *gate input for the load-bearing propagation set*, that read carries **rebuild-backed semantics** (step 4 states the rule): the authoritative set is the one the events carry, a lost map write is never allowed to shrink a propagation set; and the map claims no cross-constituent transactional consistency.
- **`consent_to_retention`** — map from `consent_id` to `{consent_record_retention_id}`. Records the Retention Window `retention_id` placed over the consent record at [Record Consent]. Its presence is records-alone evidence that the consent-record retention placement occurred (Invariant 6). **Contract classification: derived index.** *Rebuild procedure:* traverse the Audit Trail for `consent.granted` events and take `{consent_id, consent_record_retention_id}` from each payload ([Record Consent] step 6 writes both) — **the traversal is the authoritative and only rebuild source.** Enumerating the consent-record Retention Window instance and re-keying by `record_ref` is a *consistency cross-check, never a rebuild source*: a retention record with no `consent.granted` event is exactly the step-6 orphan or direct-write bypass that must stay **unresolvable** (audit-first: no resolution without the event), and the disagreement between traversal and enumeration is the orphan/bypass detector. Same three obligations — outside the atomicity surface, rebuild-on-miss, no consistency claim.

  **This map has a horizon, and the usual escape from one is deliberately closed to it.** The traversal reads `consent.granted` payloads, which the substrate destroys at the audit instance's horizon. Elsewhere in the corpus a payload-sourced rebuild that has a *second declared source* falls back to it past the horizon — [Defensible Retention](./defensible-retention.md)'s `record_to_retentions` falls back to Retention Window's own records, which over-includes and therefore can only refuse a purge. **Here the fallback exists and is forbidden, for a reason this entry already gives**: a store-sourced rebuild would resolve exactly the bypassed or step-6-orphaned `consent_id` that audit-first resolvability must leave unresolvable, and the traversal/enumeration disagreement *is* the detector. Taking the fallback would not degrade a scope claim; it would delete a security property. **So the fallback shape is unavailable wherever the audit traversal's exclusivity is itself the guarantee**, and this map is that case. What follows past the horizon is stated plainly rather than worked around: the map is not *rebuildable*, and the detector's two inputs converge — a bypassed `consent_id` and a legitimate one whose grant event has aged out become indistinguishable, because the evidence that told them apart was the payload. The deployment obligation in Configuration (`audit_trail_retention_policy`) keeps the horizon beyond the consent-record proof period; it cannot keep it beyond the consent record itself, which Consent's Invariant 8 retains for the life of the store, so every long-lived consent eventually outlives the audit horizon of its own grant event. What holds past that point is the paragraph below.

  **Past the horizon the indexes are truth-bearing, and the classification splits there.** Each of the three maps is a *derived index* while the events it derives from retain their payloads. For an entry whose source event has been purged, the map is the **only carrier** of the fact — `consent_id → {subject_ref, purpose}`, `consent_id → consent_record_retention_id`, `consent_id → {processing_scope, processor_ref, registered_at}` — because the payload is destroyed and the constituent-store fallback that could restore it is forbidden above. Such an entry is **truth-bearing under a durability obligation** (Configuration, `index_durability`): a lost entry there is not a rebuild trigger, it is a finding — for `consent_to_subject_purpose` the finding is a still-Granted consent that [Withdraw Consent] step 2 can no longer resolve, the Article 7(3) failure named above, now reached by index loss rather than by a retention setting. That half of each map is **extraction-pending** against the *Erasure Tombstone* atom *(forthcoming)* the substrate itself names for the same class of fact — a durable record of what a purge destroyed and how it was keyed; until it lands the durability is the deployment's. The split is by the source event's retention state, per entry, and a rebuild from the live trail repopulates only the derived half and leaves the truth-bearing half standing — it never treats an entry it cannot re-derive as an entry it should drop.
- **`consent_to_subject_purpose`** — map from `consent_id` to `{subject_ref, purpose}`. A convenience index so [Withdraw Consent] (which takes a `consent_id`) can name the subject and purpose on the propagation event without a Consent `read`, and so an auditor can join a `consent_id` back to the gate surface. Populated by [Record Consent]; immutable thereafter. **Contract classification: derived index.** *Rebuild procedure:* traverse the Audit Trail for `consent.granted` events and take `{consent_id, subject_ref, purpose}` from each payload — **the traversal is the authoritative and only rebuild source**; enumerating the Consent store via `read` (the same pair is immutable on every record) is a *consistency cross-check, never a rebuild source*, since a store-sourced rebuild would make a bypassed or step-6-orphaned `consent_id` resolvable at [Register Processing]/[Withdraw Consent] step 2, handing it the registration surface audit-first resolvability denies it; the traversal/enumeration disagreement is the orphan/bypass detector. Same three obligations.

  **The horizon consequence on this map is the composition's most serious, and it is a live one rather than an audit one.** [Withdraw Consent] step 2 resolves `consent_id` here and returns `rejected(not-known)` when it is absent. Past the audit instance's horizon these entries are **not rebuildable** — the rebuild finds no `consent.granted` event — the payload carrying `{consent_id, subject_ref, purpose}` is destroyed, and with it the `action_ref` the traversal filters on — so the map stays empty and the resolution fails. **A consent that genuinely exists and is still Granted in the Consent store becomes un-withdrawable**, because the audit record of its grant aged out before the consent did. That is a GDPR Article 7(3) failure — withdrawal must be as easy as grant — produced by a retention setting, and reached without any partial failure, bypass, or bug. The store-sourced fallback that would resolve it is the one this entry forbids, and forbids correctly. **The only thing standing between a deployment and this outcome is the ordering obligation in Configuration**, which is why that entry states it as an obligation with this consequence named, and why Generation acceptance check 8 treats a violation as a conformance failure rather than a defensibility preference.

The Consent store (consent records and their grant/revoke fields), the Permissions store (grants), the Retention Window store (consent-record retention records), and the Audit Trail substrate's emergent state are owned by their respective constituent instances. The composition does not duplicate them; it indexes into them via the maps above.

### Configuration

- **`consent_record_retention_policy_ref`** — the recommended Retention Window policy reference for the consent-record retention placed at [Record Consent]. The caller passes it (or a per-jurisdiction policy) as the **required** `retention_policy_ref` argument to [Record Consent], which step 5 consumes. The composition holds no default it substitutes — the argument is authoritative and required; this Configuration entry is the deployment's recommended value, not a fallback the action applies when the argument is omitted. The deployment sets this to the policy governing how long a consent record is held as demonstrability evidence (GDPR Article 7(1) burden of proof; HIPAA's six-year Authorization retention). Must resolve in the Retention Window's Policy Registry. Multi-jurisdiction policy reconciliation (selecting the longer of competing obligations) is out of scope; a Policy Reconciliation composing pattern produces the reconciled `policy_ref` this composition consumes.
- **`audit_trail_retention_policy`** — the `retention_policy` configured on this composition's single Audit Trail instance (a policy reference, or a content-derived policy selector), governing the lifetime of the *audit events* (`consent.granted`, `consent.revoked`, etc.), distinct from the consent-record retention above. It is set once on the instance; Audit Trail's `record_action` takes **no per-call retention argument**, so every audit write this composition makes inherits this configured policy. **The ordering this value must satisfy is a declared obligation, not a recommendation.** Earlier revisions said the audit trail *should* persist at least as long as the consent record it describes — advice, given for litigation defensibility, with nothing resting on it. **Three of this composition's four emergent maps rest on it**, because here the audit trail is not only evidence, it is their **only** rebuild source: `consent_to_subject_purpose`, `consent_to_retention` and `consent_to_downstream` all rebuild by traversing event payloads, and the constituent-store fallback that would otherwise cover them is deliberately forbidden (Composition state) because the traversal's exclusivity is what detects a write-ownership bypass. The obligation is: **`audit_trail_retention_policy`'s horizon outlasts the consent records it describes, including the full `consent_record_retention_policy_ref` duration.** The consequence of violating it is not a reporting gap. Past the horizon a `consent.granted` payload is destroyed, `consent_to_subject_purpose` cannot be rebuilt, and [Withdraw Consent] step 2 returns `rejected(not-known)` for a consent that genuinely exists and is still Granted — **a live, still-valid consent becomes un-withdrawable because the record of its grant aged out before the consent did**, which is a GDPR Article 7(3) failure reached with no partial failure, bypass, or bug. Litigation defensibility remains a second and independent reason for the same ordering. Generation acceptance check 8 tests it; where the consent-record retention duration sits behind a host-declared `policy_ref` this composition does not resolve, the comparison is routed to the externally-clearable set.
- **`compensation_window`** — the deployment-declared duration within which a **Revoked-but-unpropagated** consent (the revoke committed, its `consent.revoked` propagation event not landed) must have that event appended, or be escalated as an unresolved compliance finding. Invariant 3's **liveness arm is a claim about this window and is unstatable without it**, and it is also what gives the compensating retry a **terminus**: the *Cross-store consistency under partial failure* discipline retries until the event lands, which for a permanent cause is a loop indistinguishable, from the records, from an orphan nobody is working on. This is the declaring source (capability provenance) for the window Invariant 3 and Generation acceptance check 7 (*Unpropagated revocations are surfaced and compensated*) both spend. No default is prescribed: under GDPR Article 7(3) an unpropagated withdrawal is a live processing-suppression gap — though not an unbounded one here, since [Processing Permitted] reads Consent and is already honest the instant the revoke commits — and how long the *record* of it may lag is a compliance determination.
- **`reconciliation_cadence`** — the interval at which the reconciliation scan the *Cross-store consistency under partial failure* edge case mandates is run, in addition to its mandatory run at process restart. Cadence bounds *detection*, the window bounds *repair*, and a cadence longer than the window makes the window unmeetable by construction.
- **`administration_completion_bound`** — the deployment-declared maximum duration of a [Record Consent] or [Withdraw Consent] invocation between its first constituent commit and its last audit write — from `Consent.grant` to the `consent.granted` event, from `Consent.revoke` to the `consent.revoked` event — read against the seam clock the reading invocation began under. It is the **lower edge** of every reconciliation leg this composition runs or an auditor runs on its behalf: the scan and Generation acceptance checks 1 and 7 examine no store record younger than it, because a younger one may belong to an invocation still between its two writes, and a compensation fired at such a record would append beside the event the invocation is about to write — the double-propagation Invariant 3's exactly-one forbids. The **upper edge** of the same legs is `audit_trail_retention_policy`'s horizon: past it the absence of an event is destruction, not a signature (*Cross-store consistency under partial failure*). *Default:* none; a bound shorter than the slowest conforming invocation makes the scan unsafe in the direction that writes.
- **`application_actor_ref` and `application_credential`** — the composition's **service identity**: a registered actor in the substrate's Actor Identity registry under which the reconciliation scan attests every record it writes outside the invocation that owed it — the `consent.recovery_intended` record and the compensating `consent.granted` or `consent.revoked` event. The operator who performed the original act is carried in the compensating event's `data` (`granted_by` / `revoked_by`), never as its `actor_ref`: that operator's credential is not in hand when the scan runs, and attesting under it would be a false attribution the substrate would refuse or a cached credential would let through. Every action's own writes are attested by the calling operator; only the scan uses this identity.
- **`index_durability`** — the durability the deployment owes the composition's three index stores, stated as an ordering: **at least as durable as the Consent store they key**. Inside the audit horizon the indexes are derived and a lost entry is a rebuild trigger; past it their entries are the **only** carrier of facts the purge destroyed and the forbidden store fallback cannot restore (Composition state — *Past the horizon the indexes are truth-bearing*), and a lost entry there is a finding — for `consent_to_subject_purpose`, an un-withdrawable consent — rather than a miss to repair. The obligation is the deployment's until the Erasure Tombstone atom the substrate names lands.

### Primitive policies

The composition takes string-typed inputs at its action boundaries; each is validated either at this layer or by a constituent.

- **`subject_ref`** — opaque reference to the data subject. Validated by Consent's `grant` (at least one non-whitespace character); the composition propagates Consent's `invalid-request` without imposing additional rules. Not normalized, case-folded, or trimmed; equality is opaque byte-identity. *Term-collision note:* in this spec `subject_ref` always names the **data subject** (the outward authorization surface); Permissions' own spec uses "Subject Ref" for the *permission-holding internal actor* (its inward surface). At this composition's boundary that inward role is always carried by `actor_ref`, never `subject_ref` — the collision is confined to the constituent's internal vocabulary and never crosses this composition's signatures.
- **`purpose`** — opaque processing-purpose scope (`marketing:email`, `analytics:behavioral`, `hipaa:research`). Validated by Consent's `grant`. The composition does not interpret purpose semantics; purpose taxonomy governance is handled at the deployment layer (inherited from Consent's *Purpose vocabulary* edge case).
- **`consent_id`** — opaque, system-generated by Consent's `grant`. When supplied to [Register Processing] or [Withdraw Consent], it must resolve in this composition's `consent_to_subject_purpose` index — the audit-derived map the actions' step 2 consults; otherwise the action rejects with `not-known`. Resolution is deliberately *not* against the Consent instance directly: under a direct-write bypass or an unrecovered step-6 orphan the store may know a `consent_id` the events do not, and audit-first resolvability (no resolution without the `consent.granted` event; Composition state's authoritative-rebuild rule) is the contract. Used as a map key under byte-identity equality; never normalized.
- **[Processing Scope]** — opaque reference to a downstream processing activity bound to a consent (`ad-personalization`, `derived-profile-store`, `partner-share:acme`). Must contain at least one non-whitespace character; otherwise `invalid-request`. The composition does not interpret it; it is the unit enumerated in the propagation event's `affected_scopes`.
- **`processor_ref`** — opaque reference to the system or party operating the downstream processing activity. Must contain at least one non-whitespace character. Carried in `consent_to_downstream` and on the propagation event so an auditor knows *who* must act on a withdrawal.
- **`actor_ref`** — opaque internal-actor identifier for the operator performing a consent-administration action. Must contain at least one non-whitespace character; empty/whitespace-only is `invalid-request`. The substrate's Actor Identity binds it cryptographically via the paired `credential`, and it is the subject of the Permissions check.
- **`credential`** — must contain at least one non-whitespace character, checked at this layer before any write so an empty credential is refused without a substrate round-trip (`invalid-request`); beyond that it is opaque material consumed by `AuditTrail.record_action` (and, where the substrate verifies before recording, by the Actor Identity inside the substrate). The composition does not inspect it; the substrate's Actor Identity validates it at the audit write and may surface `invalid-credential`, mapped per the Action wiring preamble's uniform `record_action` rejection rule.
- **`reason`** — free-form string supplied to [Withdraw Consent] (passed to Consent's `revoke`); must contain at least one non-whitespace character (Consent's rule).
- **`retention_policy_ref`** — the consent-record policy reference supplied to [Record Consent]. Validated by Retention Window's `place_under_retention`; `invalid-policy` and `policy-not-found` from the constituent are mapped to `invalid-request` at this composition's boundary.
- **`expires_at`, `metadata`** — optional, passed through to Consent's `grant` unchanged; Consent's validation rules (`expires_at` strictly in the future; `metadata` opaque) apply.

No primitive is case-sensitivity-normalized at the composition layer; deployments wanting normalization wire it at the calling layer before invoking composition actions.

### Logic confinement (clock and id)

The clock is an **injected input at the composition's single I/O seam**, never read inside a guard or a transition and never threaded through a caller signature. Per the Logic Confinement Principle ([`execution-contract.md`](../execution-contract.md)), the host reads the clock once per invocation and injects `now` (`clock_t`) at the seam before the orchestration runs; the actions below are pure functions of the stored records plus that injected `now`. Because the clock enters at the seam rather than as a parameter, the action signatures below carry **no** `now` argument — the same discipline Retention Window pins for `place_under_retention`.

`now` is consumed for exactly one purpose at this layer:

- **Stamping immutable timestamps** on a committed write — `registered_at`, carried on the `processing.registered` event and on the `consent_to_downstream` binding at [Register Processing]; `revoked_at`, carried on the `consent.revoked` propagation event at [Withdraw Consent]; and `intended_at`, carried on the `consent.grant_intended` and `consent.withdrawal_intended` events at [Record Consent] and [Withdraw Consent]. Nothing else in this composition consumes the clock: it computes **no derived deadline** (`expires_at` is caller-supplied and passed through to Consent's `grant` unchanged — the composition performs no clock arithmetic on it), and it evaluates **no clock-bearing guard** of its own.

**The time-sensitive predicate is the constituent's, not this composition's.** [Processing Permitted] is a point-in-time question — *is there valid consent for this subject and purpose right now?* — but the composition does not answer it by comparing timestamps. It delegates to `Consent.check`, which resolves `granted` versus `expired` against the `now` injected at **Consent's own seam**. This composition adds no second clock read and no predicate of its own on top of the constituent's answer.

**No stored consent-validity flag.** The composition's emergent state (`consent_to_downstream`, `consent_to_retention`, `consent_to_subject_purpose`) holds bindings, retention ids, and index entries — never a derived "valid", "expired", or "still-consented" boolean. Consent validity is a read-time projection produced by `Consent.check` at the moment it is asked, so nothing at this layer can lag the clock (the same discipline Retention Window pins in its Invariant 11). Revocation is a recorded state transition in Consent, not a clock-derived flag this composition maintains.

**One injected `now` per invocation, shared.** Within a single [Register Processing] call, the `registered_at` on the `processing.registered` event and the `registered_at` on the binding added to `consent_to_downstream` are the **same** injected reading, so the audited registration act and the map entry it grounds carry one instant rather than two adjacent ones — which is what makes Invariant 4's event-to-binding correspondence exact. The same rule governs the intent records, and it reaches the two pairs differently — a distinction worth stating rather than smoothing over. At [Withdraw Consent] the `intended_at` on the intent event, the `revoked_at` passed explicitly to `Consent.revoke`, and the `revoked_at` on the propagation event are **one** injected reading; the pass-through is what makes that true, and their order therefore cannot be read off their stamps, because the stamps are equal by construction. At [Record Consent] the situation is different and weaker: the `consent.granted` event carries **no timestamp of its own** for the intent event's stamp to be compared against, and the Consent record's `granted_at` is stamped from the reading injected at **Consent's own seam** — the atom takes no clock parameter, so this composition cannot make those two readings one and does not claim to. **The consequence is the same either way, for different reasons:** ordering between an intent event and its outcome is read off the Event Log `sequence_number` — the only order source that spans them — and no check may compare their timestamps. This is not a weakness of the stamps; it is why the composition has never depended on them for order. Ids come from the constituents' seams: `consent_id` is minted by Consent's `grant`, `retention_id` by Retention Window's `place_under_retention`, and `event_id` by Audit Trail's `record_action`. This composition introduces no identifier of its own, mints none inside a transition, and generates no cryptographic material.

### Action wiring

The composition exposes five actions. Four are state-changing and record in the Audit Trail; the fifth, [Processing Permitted], is a read-only gate query and records nothing (a read-only query produces no state change, so an audit event would be a false positive in the action record). Every constituent rejection is mapped — propagated, renamed, or surfaced as a new code — at the composition's boundary; silent rejection-code drift is a reference-graph finding.

For every `AuditTrail.record_action` call below, the substrate's rejection taxonomy (`invalid-credential | invalid-request | recording-failure(step)` — the constituent's failure arm carries the failed step as its payload) is mapped uniformly. **The `(step)` payload is load-bearing for recovery:** a `recording-failure` whose step shows the Event Log append itself did not land is retryable as a fresh `record_action`; the step-4 arm — retention placement over the *already-appended* audit event failed — leaves the event in the log, appended and attested, so a blind re-record would double-append. That arm is never retried at this layer: the substrate's own Invariant-2 reconciliation owns the retention-arm gap, and this composition treats the event as landed — **which means the action proceeds**: at a post-commit record the `step-4` arm populates the maps, adds the binding, or returns the result exactly as a landed event does, with a hard alert on the unretained event; at an intent record it stops as every intent arm does, leaving an intent event whose lack of an outcome is the residue Generation acceptance check 6 already triages. **`invalid-request` is never unreachable at any `record_action`, and it is a deployment fault at every one of them:** step-2 and step-3 validation foreclose the caller-input source, but the substrate routes its own retention-configuration faults (Retention Window's `invalid-policy` / `policy-not-found` at the substrate's step 4) onto the same arm — reached with the event **already appended**. The token does not say which source fired, so the arm's landing is the same pre-checked recovery discipline as `recording-failure`'s (*Cross-store consistency under partial failure*): the invocation returns `rejected(invalid-request)` with a hard alert naming the two possible causes, and the scan's pre-check — traverse before compensating — is what tells an appended event from an absent one. **Authentication precedes commitment.** All four administration actions take a `credential`, and the substrate validates it against the actor registry's public material for the supplied `actor_ref` **inside** `record_action` — so where an action's first `record_action` sits in its step list decides whether an unverified caller can move state. Two of the four already had it first and need no further mechanism: [Register Processing] records its event before adding the binding, and [Read Consent History] records its access event after a pure read that commits nothing, so at both an unverified credential is refused before any state exists to be wrong. The other two did not. [Record Consent] granted a consent and placed it under retention, and [Withdraw Consent] performed a **terminal, non-retroactive** revocation, before the credential reached any write that would check it. Both now open with an **intent record** — an `AuditTrail.record_action` naming what the invocation is about to do, written before the `Consent` call that commits — so `invalid-credential` is a clean pre-state refusal at all four actions, with nothing committed, and each outcome event carries `intent_event_id` back to its intent event for an exact per-invocation join.

**No deployment pre-check is owed, and none is named.** Actor Identity is reached only transitively, through the substrate — but the composition reaches that surface at whatever point it chooses to write, so writing before the state change is entirely within its own boundary. This composition therefore performs the check itself at every action that takes a credential, and pushes no authentication obligation to the deployment. (The Permissions check it performs gates *authority*, which is a different question from *credential validity*; the two are kept distinct by Invariant 7 and are now both anchored to the same verified actor.)

**What this makes sound beyond the ordering.** Each administration action's step 1 is `Permissions.permitted(actor_ref, …)`, which takes an actor reference and **no credential** — so Invariant 7's guarantee that an action was authorized was being asserted over an identity nobody had yet verified, and a caller who merely knew a privileged `actor_ref` passed it. The intent record does not move the Permissions check; it establishes, before anything commits, that the caller is the actor whose authority that check consulted. The authenticated principal and the principal whose authority the guarantee asserts are now the same principal — the half of the rule that ordering alone does not deliver.

**What the ordering deliberately leaves outside, and why.** The Permissions check stays at step 1, *ahead* of the intent record, so a `permission-denied` refusal costs one store read and writes nothing. The consequence is that a denied attempt is unattributed and unrecorded — any caller may probe whether an arbitrary `actor_ref` holds `consent:revoke` and leave no trace. That is a deliberate boundary, not an oversight: recording every denied probe would make an unauthenticated caller the author of audit-trail volume, and failed-attempt logging is a distinct matter with its own retention and alerting semantics, belonging to a composing failed-attempt pattern rather than to this one. What this composition guarantees is that no attempt which *changes anything* is unattributed; it does not guarantee that every attempt is recorded.

**What a validation does not establish.** A successful validation establishes that material matching the actor's registered verifier was presented at that instant. It does *not* establish that the presenter **is** that actor (a stolen credential validates), that the presentation is bound to a channel or session, or that it cannot be replayed. It establishes nothing whatever about the **data subject** — the `subject_ref` is never authenticated here, and the question of whether the subject's consent was freely given and informed remains an externally-clearable check. Operator authentication and subject consent are the two authorization surfaces Invariant 7 keeps apart; verifying the first says nothing about the second.

#### `record_consent`

```
record_consent(
 actor_ref,
 subject_ref,
 purpose,
 credential,
 retention_policy_ref,
 expires_at?,
 metadata?
) →
  consent_id
 | rejected(
   permission-denied
  | invalid-credential
  | invalid-request
  | recording-failure
  )
```

Records a data subject's consent for a purpose under an operator's authority, places the consent record under retention, and audits the grant.

Steps:

1. `Permissions.permitted(actor_ref, "consent:grant")` → if `denied`, return `rejected(permission-denied)`. No constituent state has changed. Stop.
2. Validate `actor_ref`, `credential`, `subject_ref`, `purpose`, and `retention_policy_ref` per Primitive policies — non-emptiness only; the *semantic* validation of `retention_policy_ref` against the Policy Registry is Retention Window's at step 5, and the residue differs by where the rejection lands: refused here, nothing exists; refused at step 5, a `consent.grant_intended` event and a Granted, unretained consent do. Any failure → `rejected(invalid-request)`. Stop.
3. **Intent record.** `AuditTrail.record_action(action_ref=consent.grant_intended, actor_ref, credential, data={subject_ref, purpose, retention_policy_ref, expires_at?, intended_at = now})` → `event_id_intent`. This is where the substrate validates the caller's credential against the actor registry for the supplied `actor_ref`. Nothing has committed, so every arm is a clean pre-state rejection: `invalid-credential` → `rejected(invalid-credential)`; `invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`, a deployment fault (Action wiring); `recording-failure(step)` → `rejected(recording-failure)`, retryable as a fresh intent where the step shows the append did not land, and leaving a standing intent event on the `step-4` arm — expected residue either way. Stop on any. The payload carries **no `consent_id`** — none exists yet, and it must never be made to carry one: `consent_id` is the key all three derived indexes are built on, and `consent.granted` is their sole authoritative rebuild source, so an intent event naming a `consent_id` would be precisely the resolvable-without-a-grant-event bypass the audit-first rule forecloses. The rebuild procedures traverse `consent.granted` and never this event.
4. Call `Consent.grant(subject_ref, purpose, granted_by=actor_ref, expires_at?, metadata?)` → `consent_id` (or map: `invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`; `storage-failure` → `rejected(recording-failure)`. Stop on either — no retention placed, no audit written).
5. Place the consent record under **consent-record retention**: `Retention Window.place_under_retention(record_ref=consent_id, policy_ref=retention_policy_ref)` → `consent_record_retention_id` (the per-call argument is authoritative and required — the action substitutes nothing; the configured `consent_record_retention_policy_ref` is the deployment's *recommended* value, which a single-policy deployment simply passes on every call). Map `invalid-policy`/`policy-not-found`/`invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`; `storage-failure` → `rejected(recording-failure)`. Stop on either — if stopped here, the consent grant from step 4 persists as a Granted record with no retention placement and no `consent.granted` event — the step-3 `consent.grant_intended` event stands, since it is never withdrawn — surfaced per the *Cross-store consistency under partial failure* edge case.
6. Record the grant — audit-first with respect to **resolvability**, not write order (steps 4–5 have already committed constituent state; what this discipline guarantees is that the maps are not populated until the event lands, so no `consent_id` becomes resolvable without its `consent.granted` event): `AuditTrail.record_action(action_ref=consent.granted, actor_ref, credential, data={intent_event_id: event_id_intent, consent_id, subject_ref, purpose, expires_at?, consent_record_retention_id})` → `event_id`. Map the arms rather than collapsing them: `invalid-credential` → `rejected(invalid-credential)` — reachable here only where the actor's registration changed between step 3 and this write (a revocation or a key rotation mid-invocation), and surfaced as itself so an operator does not go looking for a malformed payload; `recording-failure(step-4)` → the event **is** appended and attested: proceed to step 7 and return normally, with a hard alert on the unretained event (Action wiring); `invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`, a deployment fault whose retention source leaves this event appended — the scan's pre-check decides; `recording-failure(step-2 | step-3)` → `rejected(recording-failure)`. On any of the three that stop, after steps 4–5 succeeded, **no composition maps are populated**, so no `consent_id` becomes resolvable by a downstream composition action without its `consent.granted` event having landed; the implementation surfaces the orphan per the *Cross-store consistency under partial failure* edge case.
7. Populate composition state only after the grant event has landed: `consent_to_retention[consent_id] = {consent_record_retention_id}` and `consent_to_subject_purpose[consent_id] = {subject_ref, purpose}`. (`consent_to_downstream[consent_id]` begins empty; bindings are added by [Register Processing].)
8. Return `consent_id`.

#### `register_processing`

```
register_processing(
 actor_ref,
 consent_id,
 processing_scope,
 processor_ref,
 credential
) →
  registered
 | rejected(
   permission-denied
  | not-known
  | invalid-credential
  | invalid-request
  | recording-failure
  )
```

Binds a downstream processing activity to a consent, so that a later withdrawal can enumerate it. This is the action that makes "what relied on this consent" a recorded fact rather than tribal knowledge. It does not check consent *state* — a processing activity may be registered against a Granted consent before processing begins; whether processing may actually proceed is the [Processing Permitted] gate's job at processing time.

Steps:

1. `Permissions.permitted(actor_ref, "consent:register-processing")` → if `denied`, `rejected(permission-denied)`. Stop.
2. Resolve `consent_id` in `consent_to_subject_purpose`. If absent → `rejected(not-known)` (the consent is unknown to this composition — it was never recorded through [Record Consent], or the id is wrong). Stop.
3. Validate `actor_ref`, `credential`, `processing_scope`, `processor_ref` per Primitive policies. Any failure → `rejected(invalid-request)`. Stop.
4. Record: `AuditTrail.record_action(action_ref=processing.registered, actor_ref, credential, data={consent_id, processing_scope, processor_ref, registered_at = now})` → `event_id`, where `registered_at` is stamped from the seam-injected `now` for this invocation (see *Logic confinement*). If this fails, map per the preamble's pre-state rule — **this write is itself the credential check for this action**, and nothing has committed before it, which is why [Register Processing] needs no separate intent record: `invalid-credential` → `rejected(invalid-credential)`, surfaced as itself rather than folded into `invalid-request`, since the two call for different remediations (re-authenticate versus fix the payload); `recording-failure(step-4)` → the event is appended: add the binding (step 5) and return `registered`, with a hard alert on the unretained event (Action wiring); `invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`, a deployment fault whose retention source leaves the event appended — the binding is then a map entry the rebuild recovers (step 5's audit-first rule holds: the event exists), and a caller's retry lands a lawful re-registration; `recording-failure(step-2 | step-3)` → `rejected(recording-failure)`, retryable as a fresh registration. On any arm that stops **the binding is not added to the map** (audit-first: the registration is not resolvable without its event). Stop.
5. Add the binding only after the event has landed: `consent_to_downstream[consent_id] ∪= {processing_scope, processor_ref}` — set union on the pair identity: a first registration adds the element with `registered_at` set from the **same seam-injected `now`** the step-4 event carries (one instant, not two readings); a re-registration of an existing pair leaves the element and its `registered_at` untouched.
6. Return `registered`. (Re-registering an identical `{processing_scope, processor_ref}` against the same consent is idempotent at the set level — the set absorbs the duplicate — but still records a `processing.registered` event, because the registration *act* is itself an auditable decision; the set membership, not the event count, is what the propagation enumerates.)

#### `withdraw_consent`

```
withdraw_consent(
 actor_ref,
 consent_id,
 credential,
 reason
) →
  withdrawn
 | rejected(
   permission-denied
  | not-known
  | already-revoked
  | already-expired
  | invalid-credential
  | invalid-request
  | recording-failure
  )
```

The single emergent action that revokes a consent **and propagates the withdrawal** — recording, atomically with the revocation, the complete set of downstream processing scopes the consent governed. This is the composition's structural reason to exist; the propagation event is what makes a withdrawal's downstream impact a records-alone fact rather than a hope that every processing system re-checks the consent store.

Steps:

1. `Permissions.permitted(actor_ref, "consent:revoke")` → if `denied`, `rejected(permission-denied)`. Stop. (GDPR Article 7(3) requires withdrawal be *as easy as grant*; that ease is a property of the data-subject-facing surface upstream. The Permissions check here gates the *operator* recording the withdrawal on the subject's behalf — it does not gate the subject's right to withdraw, which is absolute; a deployment exposing self-service withdrawal wires the subject's own request to an actor with `consent:revoke` authority at the calling layer.)
2. Resolve `consent_id` in `consent_to_subject_purpose`. If absent → `rejected(not-known)`. Stop. Resolve `{subject_ref, purpose}`.
3. Validate `actor_ref`, `credential`, `reason` per Primitive policies. Any failure → `rejected(invalid-request)`. Stop.
4. **Compute the propagation set first, from the records:** read `affected_scopes = consent_to_downstream[consent_id]` (the complete set of `{processing_scope, processor_ref}` bindings registered against this consent; empty if none). This read precedes the revoke so the set recorded is exactly the set the records held at withdrawal time; the step-5 intent event, appended next and inside the same serialized invocation, is what makes *withdrawal time* a fixed point in the log rather than a wall-clock phrase — see step 5 and Generation acceptance check 2. **The read is rebuild-backed** (Composition state): `consent_to_downstream` is a derived index over the `processing.registered` events, and the authoritative set is the one the events carry — an implementation must ensure the set it records equals the event-derived set (rebuilding the index before the read where its integrity is in doubt), so a lost map write after a registration event landed is a rebuild trigger and can never shrink a propagation set. Generation acceptance check 2 verifies exactly this equality from the records.
5. **Intent record.** `AuditTrail.record_action(action_ref=consent.withdrawal_intended, actor_ref, credential, data={consent_id, subject_ref, purpose, reason, intended_at = now})` → `event_id_intent`, written before the constituent call that commits — which is where the revoker's credential is verified, so a **terminal, non-retroactive** revocation is never performed on an unverified claim (*Authentication precedes commitment*; Invariant 8). Nothing has committed, so every arm is a clean pre-state rejection: `invalid-credential` → `rejected(invalid-credential)`; `invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`, a deployment fault (Action wiring); `recording-failure(step)` → `rejected(recording-failure)`, retryable as a fresh intent where the step shows the append did not land, and leaving a standing intent event on the `step-4` arm — the case (iii) residue of Generation acceptance check 6. Stop on any. The payload deliberately carries **no `affected_scopes`**: by Invariant 3 the propagation set has exactly one authoritative carrier, and a second copy on a second event would hand an auditor a set to rebuild from that check 2's equality is not stated against. The intent event names *that* a withdrawal was intended for this consent, never *what* it will enumerate. **It does, however, fix the boundary of what will be enumerated, and that is its second job.** Nothing can be registered against this consent between the step-4 read and this append — both sit inside one serialized invocation (*Concurrency*) — so `affected_scopes` is exactly the `processing.registered` bindings ordered before *this* event, which is the definition Generation acceptance check 2 uses and the one a compensating append re-derives the set from. Carrying a boundary rather than a copy of the set is what lets the one-authoritative-carrier rule above hold and the recovery still have something to work from.
6. Call `Consent.revoke(consent_id, revoked_by=actor_ref, reason, revoked_at=now)` — the seam-injected reading for this invocation is passed explicitly through the constituent's optional `revoked_at?` parameter, so the atom's stored `revoked_at` and this composition's audit stamp are one reading rather than two taken at two seams → `revoked` (or map: `not-known` → `rejected(recording-failure)` — an internal-consistency anomaly, since step 2 resolved the id in this composition's own map but Consent does not know it. **This arm carries no retry semantics**: the `(step)`-aware retry discipline addresses a substrate write that did not land, and nothing here failed to land. It resolves as case (iv) of Generation acceptance check 6 — a standing finding against the index and the store, not a compensation case; `already-revoked` → `rejected(already-revoked)`; `already-expired` → `rejected(already-expired)`; `invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`; `storage-failure` → `rejected(recording-failure)`. Stop on any — no propagation event is recorded if the revoke did not occur).
7. **Append the propagation event, after the revoke** (the binding — see *The load-bearing wiring decision*; the ordering is what earns Invariant 3's first safety arm, and it is deliberate: the revoke is terminal, so an event appended after it can never be left naming a revocation that did not happen): `AuditTrail.record_action(action_ref=consent.revoked, actor_ref, credential, data={intent_event_id: event_id_intent, consent_id, subject_ref, purpose, reason, affected_scopes, revoked_at = now})` → `event_id`, where `revoked_at` is stamped from the seam-injected `now` for this invocation (see *Logic confinement*) — the same reading step 6 passed to `Consent.revoke` as its `revoked_at`, so the atom's stored value and this event carry one instant. That is a **pass-through**, not an atomicity claim: the two writes are separate and neither is reversible, and passing the reading rather than re-reading it is precisely how one instant survives being written twice. This is the authoritative withdrawal time for an Article 7(3) dispute; it is one value precisely because it is passed rather than re-read. The `affected_scopes` set on this event is the complete enumeration computed in step 4. Map the arms rather than collapsing them: `invalid-credential` → `rejected(invalid-credential)`, reachable only where the actor's registration changed between step 5 and this write; `recording-failure(step-4)` → the propagation event **is** appended and attested: return `withdrawn`, with a hard alert on the unretained event (Action wiring) — nothing is owed and nothing may be retried; `invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`, a deployment fault whose retention source leaves this event appended; `recording-failure(step-2 | step-3)` → `rejected(recording-failure)`. On any of the three that stop, after step 6 succeeded, the consent is Revoked in Consent and the propagation **may** be unattested — which arm, and the scan's pre-check, decide: the recovery is **`(step)`-aware and pre-checked** (Action wiring preamble; *Cross-store consistency under partial failure*). Before any compensating write, the recovery traverses the log for an existing `consent.revoked` event carrying this `consent_id` — the substrate's step-4 arm leaves the event already appended and attested, and a second append would violate Invariant 3's exactly-one and check 2 — and only where no event landed is the `record_action` retried until it lands (the orphan surfaced as a high-priority finding meanwhile; the compensating event carries `cascade_recovery = true`). **There is no host transaction boundary over this pair and there could not be** — Consent's revocation is immutable once committed and the substrate's append cannot be withdrawn, so neither write is enlistable (Edge cases — *Cross-store consistency under partial failure*). What makes the pair sound is the **order**, not a transaction: the event follows the revoke, so a propagation record never outruns the revocation it reports, and the one reachable gap runs the recoverable way (Invariant 3).
8. Return `withdrawn`. The action's guarantee is that the consent is revoked **and** its complete downstream scope set is recorded in a tamper-evident propagation event — not that any downstream processor has yet acted on the withdrawal. Downstream processors consume the propagation event (or re-query [Processing Permitted]) to cease processing; performing the cessation (deleting derived data, halting a pipeline) is the processor's obligation, signalled but not performed here.

#### `read_consent_history`

```
read_consent_history(
 actor_ref,
 subject_ref,
 credential
) →
  ordered_sequence_of_consents
 | rejected(
   permission-denied
  | invalid-credential
  | invalid-request
  | recording-failure
  )
```

The permission-gated read surface for compliance dashboards, DSAR (Data Subject Access Request) workflows, and regulatory reporting. Reading a data subject's consent history is itself a regulated act under several regimes, so it produces a meta-event.

Steps:

1. `Permissions.permitted(actor_ref, "consent:read")` → if `denied`, `rejected(permission-denied)`. Stop.
2. Validate `actor_ref`, `credential`, `subject_ref` per Primitive policies. Any failure → `rejected(invalid-request)`. Stop.
3. Call `Consent.read({subject_ref})` → ordered sequence of consent records (Consent orders by `granted_at` then `consent_id`). Consent's `read` rejects only on malformed query, and step 2's validation makes this composition's one query shape well-formed by construction — but the arm is mapped rather than left dangling: should `invalid-query` nonetheless surface (defense in depth against an implementation drift), it maps to `rejected(invalid-request)`.
4. Record the access (meta-event): `AuditTrail.record_action(action_ref=consent.history-read, actor_ref, credential, data={subject_ref, record_count})` → `event_id`. If this fails, map per the preamble's pre-state rule (the read is simply not returned) — this write is the action's credential check and no separate intent record is owed. **One committing write is nonetheless reachable at step 3, and it is named rather than glossed over:** a Consent instance implementing expiry lazily *must* write the `Expired` stored-state transition inside the `read` that first evaluates a record past its `expires_at`, before returning (Consent Invariant 6 and its *Expiry implementation* edge case), so an unauthenticated caller can occasion that write. It is outside Invariant 8 by the rule's own third answer rather than by exception: the transition materializes a value already true of the record by its own stored deadline, carries no caller-supplied content, names no actor, and would be identical whoever asked — it relies on no actor's authority, so there is no principal to authenticate for it. What it does expose is that a `read` can be occasioned before authentication; the arms below are what refuse the *result*: `invalid-credential` → `rejected(invalid-credential)`, surfaced as itself; `recording-failure(step-4)` → the access event is appended: return the records, with a hard alert on the unretained event (Action wiring); `invalid-request` → `rejected(invalid-request)`, a deployment fault — where its retention source has appended the access event, the trail records an access whose result this composition refused to return, which is the safe direction and is stated so an auditor does not read it as a leak; `recording-failure(step-2 | step-3)` → `rejected(recording-failure)`. On any arm that stops the read result is not returned, because returning regulated records without recording the access would defeat the access-trail obligation. (This is the one place a read is gated on an audit write — deliberately, because under the regimes this composition serves, access to consent records is itself auditable.)
5. Return the consent records.

#### `processing_permitted`

```
processing_permitted(subject_ref, purpose) →
  permitted
 | rejected(
   invalid-request
  | not-permitted(state)  // state ∈ {revoked, expired, not-known}
  )
```

The read-only consent-gates-processing query. **No Audit Trail record is produced** — this is a read-only query that changes no state, and recording it would falsely populate the action record with non-actions. Processing systems consume this query as their single consent gate; they must not read Consent state directly (see Edge cases — *Processing systems reading Consent directly*).

Steps:

1. Validate `subject_ref` and `purpose` per Primitive policies (each at least one non-whitespace character). Any failure → `rejected(invalid-request)`. Stop. This is load-bearing for the signal's honesty: `Consent.check` is a pure query that never rejects, so without this step a malformed `subject_ref` would flow through to `not-known` — telling the caller *this subject never consented* when the truth is *your input is malformed*, two different remediations (fix the caller vs request consent).
2. Call `Consent.check(subject_ref, purpose)` — the point-in-time evaluation is the constituent's, resolved against the `now` injected at **Consent's own seam**; this composition reads no clock here and passes no `at_time` (see *Logic confinement*) → one of `granted | revoked | expired | not-known`.
3. If `granted` → return `permitted`.
4. Otherwise → return `rejected(not-permitted(state))`, naming the actual `check` result: `revoked` (consent was withdrawn), `expired` (consent's time bound elapsed), or `not-known` (no consent record for this subject and purpose). The named state lets the calling processing system distinguish *never consented* from *withdrawn* from *lapsed* — different remediation paths (request consent, honor the withdrawal, request renewal).

### The load-bearing wiring decision — consent-gates-processing with revocation propagation

The composition's structural reason to exist: **a system processes personal data on a consent basis only through the [Processing Permitted] gate, which is `permitted` exactly when valid consent exists; and a withdrawal commits the Consent revocation and a propagation event enumerating every downstream scope the consent governed — together or not at all.**

*Principle.* GDPR Articles 6–7 require that consent-based processing have a valid consent at processing time, and Article 7(3) (with Article 17(1)(b)) requires that withdrawal end the basis for future processing — which, operationally, means the systems relying on that consent must learn of the withdrawal. Structurally: no consent-based processing may proceed for a subject/purpose without valid consent, and the system must be able to prove — from the records alone — both that the gate was the single consent precondition and that every withdrawal's downstream impact was completely enumerated and sealed at the moment of revocation.

*Likely objection.* Consent already owns the `granted/revoked/expired` lifecycle and already exposes `check`. Why not have each processing system call `Consent.check` directly, and why does withdrawal need a separate propagation event when the revocation is already in the Consent store? The atom already records the revocation.

*Mechanism that resolves it.* Consent owns the state but exposes no gate and no propagation — by design. Its specification states that it *"does not enforce processing suppression"* and names downstream propagation as out of scope. Two gaps follow. First, if each processing system calls `Consent.check` directly, the gate is re-implemented per system, and the first system that forgets the check, caches a stale result, or applies a subtly different predicate (e.g., treats `expired` as `granted`) breaks the Article 6 basis silently and per-system; centralizing the gate at [Processing Permitted] makes the precondition exist exactly once. Second — and this is the half no constituent can supply — the Consent store records *that* a consent was revoked, but it does not record *what was relying on it*: Consent has no notion of downstream processing activities, because importing that notion would break its freestanding status. So a `Consent.revoke` alone leaves the withdrawal's downstream impact unrecorded; a processing system that never re-checks simply keeps processing, and no record exists of which systems *should* have stopped. The composition closes this by maintaining the `consent_to_downstream` registry (populated by [Register Processing], an act that is itself audited) and by making [Withdraw Consent] record the complete `affected_scopes` set on the `consent.revoked` event. **What binds the two is the order, not a transaction, and stating that correctly matters because the alternative is a claim nothing can honor.** Neither write is reversible — Consent's revocation is terminal by its own invariant, and the substrate's append cannot be withdrawn — so no host transaction can enlist the pair, and an earlier revision of this section asserted one anyway. The event follows the revoke deliberately, which buys the guarantee an auditor reads in the direction they read it: a propagation record always has a real revocation behind it, unconditionally. The gap that remains runs the other way — a revocation whose propagation record has not yet landed — and it is a gap in the *accounting*, not in the *suppression*, because [Processing Permitted] reads the Consent store and answers `not-permitted(revoked)` from the instant the revoke commits. It is surfaced, bounded by a declared `compensation_window`, and compensated with a `cascade_recovery = true` marker (Invariant 3; Edge cases — *Cross-store consistency under partial failure*). What would genuinely defeat the purpose is the other partial — a propagation event with no revocation behind it, an assertion that a withdrawal was honored when the store says it was not — and the ordering makes that one unreachable.

*Result.* The gate is structural and centralized; the propagation is complete and sealed *over the registered set*. An auditor can verify, from the records alone, that every binding in the registry traces to a `processing.registered` event, and that for every withdrawn consent a `consent.revoked` event enumerates exactly the downstream scopes that were registered — so the question *"when this person withdrew consent, what was supposed to stop, and is it recorded?"* is answered by reading one sealed event, not by interviewing every processing system. **What the records cannot prove is registration completeness in the outward direction:** the composition proves what *was* registered, never that every real-world processing activity *was registered at all* — an unregistered processor is invisible to the propagation, exactly as a gate-bypassing one is invisible to Invariant 1(b). Registration completeness is therefore the deployment's second single-surface obligation, paired with the gate obligation and named in Edge cases (*Registration completeness is a deployment obligation*). The single-surface discipline (one gate query, one registration surface, one propagation event class) is the records-alone-defensible signal, conditional on both obligations being honored.

## Composition-level invariants

These invariants emerge from the composition. None belongs to a single constituent; each requires two or more working together to hold.

- **Invariant 1 — Consent gates processing.** Two claims, distinguished by what enforces each. *(a) Query result — composition-enforced:* `processing_permitted(subject_ref, purpose)` returns `permitted` if and only if `Consent.check(subject_ref, purpose)` at the current time returns `granted`. *Defended in-line:* [Processing Permitted] step 3 returns `permitted` only on `granted`; every other `check` result returns `rejected(not-permitted(state))` — the biconditional is exact and the composition enforces it. *(b) Gating property — deployment-conditional:* a processing system reaches a consent-based action only through this gate, which holds exactly when processing systems consume [Processing Permitted] rather than reading Consent directly. This is the one structural obligation pushed to deployment (named in Edge cases — *Processing systems reading Consent directly*); a system that bypasses the gate is a composition-bypass finding, not a valid path. The "iff" is a property of the query result; the gate guarantee additionally requires the single-surface obligation to be honored.

- **Invariant 2 — Grant audit coverage.** Every [Record Consent] that grants a consent produces a `consent.granted` Audit Trail event carrying the `consent_id`, attributed and tamper-evident via the substrate seal. *Rests on:* Consent's return of `consent_id` on `grant`, [Record Consent] step 6, and Audit Trail's attribution and seal coverage (Audit Trail Invariants 1 and 3). A Granted consent reachable through this composition with no `consent.granted` event is a conformance failure.

- **Invariant 3 — Revocation propagation completeness (load-bearing).** Every [Withdraw Consent] that revokes a consent produces exactly one `consent.revoked` Audit Trail event whose `affected_scopes` set equals the complete set of downstream bindings registered against that `consent_id` in `consent_to_downstream` at revocation time, **These two writes do not commit together or not at all, and no host transaction boundary makes them.** This pattern's own *Cross-store consistency under partial failure* entry states the refutation two sections away from where the claim was made — *"Consent's revocation is immutable once committed, so synchronous rollback is not available"* — and the same is true of the other member: the propagation event is appended through a substrate that declares an appended event cannot be withdrawn. **Neither half of the pair can be taken back, so there was never a transaction to enlist them in**, and describing the gap as arising *"where the host cannot provide that atomicity"* named as an exception what is in fact the only case. **The honest claim therefore splits, and the write order is what makes the split favourable:** [Withdraw Consent] revokes at step 6 and appends the propagation event at step 7, deliberately, so the reachable partial is always the recoverable one.

  - **Safety — no `consent.revoked` propagation event without its committed revocation.** This holds unconditionally, and it is earned twice over: the event is appended only after `Consent.revoke` has returned `revoked`, and that revocation is **terminal** by the constituent's own invariant (Consent Invariants 3, 9), so it cannot later un-commit beneath an event that names it. An auditor may read any `consent.revoked` event as a real withdrawal without qualification, which is the direction an Article 7(3) dispute runs.
  - **Safety — no *unsurfaced* unpropagated revocation.** The reverse partial — a Revoked consent whose propagation event never landed — **is reachable and durable**. It is never silent. A failure that *returns* surfaces it in the same outcome that returns `rejected(recording-failure)`; a failure that *cannot* return is caught by the reconciliation scan the *Cross-store consistency under partial failure* edge case mandates, which enumerates Revoked consents against `consent.revoked` events and triages each through the five cases Generation acceptance check 6 already defines — including the case that forbids compensating a direct-write bypass.
  - **Liveness — every unpropagated revocation is propagated within the declared `compensation_window`, and a compensated withdrawal is distinguishable from a clean one.** The append is retried until it lands, under the `(step)`-aware pre-check that forbids a second event; the compensating event carries `cascade_recovery = true`. The window is what gives that retry a terminus: past it the orphan escalates as an unresolved finding rather than remaining an open loop.
  - **What is *not* at risk during the gap, and saying so is the point of the ordering.** Processing suppression is not deferred by an unpropagated revocation: [Processing Permitted] reads the Consent store, so it answers `not-permitted(revoked)` from the instant step 6 commits. What is missing during the window is the *record* of which downstream scopes should stop — an accountability gap, not a lawfulness one. This is why this pattern's reachable partial is the recoverable kind, and it is the discriminator that made this a restatement rather than the protocol repair [Capability-Backed Sharing](./capability-backed-sharing.md) needed.

  *At quiescence, with no open compensation, the binding is bijective exactly as stated: revoke ⇔ complete propagation record.* *Defended in-line:* [Withdraw Consent] computes `affected_scopes` from the records before the revoke (step 4), records its intent under the caller's credential (step 5), revokes (step 6), and appends the propagation event (step 7). The step-5 intent event stands **before** the revocation: it is the write whose execution verified the revoker, so it necessarily precedes the revocation it authorizes, and it is not withdrawn when that revocation does not follow — an invocation that records its intent and is then refused `already-revoked` leaves an intent event with no propagation event, which is expected residue and not a violation of this invariant, whose quantifier runs over consents revoked, not over withdrawals intended. **And the intent event pays for itself a second time: it is the propagation set's boundary marker.** *At revocation time* needs a fixed point, and the one check 2 used to use — *ordered before the `consent.revoked` event in the log* — is not fixed, because a compensating event lands in a later invocation than the read it reports. [Register Processing] is deliberately permitted against an already-Revoked consent, and the serialization obligation binds the two *invocations*, not the span from a step-4 read to a compensating append, so a registration landing during a recovery window sits before the compensating event and outside its set — and the old boundary convicted a correct compensation. The boundary is therefore the withdrawal's own **`consent.withdrawal_intended` event**, which the propagation event already carries as `intent_event_id`: nothing can be registered between the step-4 read and that append, because both are inside one serialized invocation. This costs **no new field on any event and no second copy of the set** — the deliberate one-authoritative-carrier decision at step 5 stands untouched — and it makes the set **re-derivable** during compensation, since `affected_scopes` is exactly the `processing.registered` bindings ordered before that intent event. Which is what a compensation needs and could not previously get: the set existed only in the event that failed to land and the process that died. *Rests on:* Consent's `revoke` (Consent Invariants 3, 9 — terminal, non-retroactive), which is also what earns the first safety arm; **Audit Trail's append-only contract — that an appended event cannot be withdrawn and offers no synchronous rollback — the declared source of the boundary this invariant is organized around, and the capability whose absence the earlier all-or-nothing claim was silently assuming**; the `consent_to_downstream` registry's completeness (it is never silently pruned — Composition state) and its groundedness in `processing.registered` events (Invariant 4), which is what makes the set derivable from the log rather than only from this composition's index; the composition's **revoke-then-append** ordering and its **intent-event boundary**; the host's per-`consent_id` serialization obligation (Edge cases — *Concurrency*); and the deployment-declared **`compensation_window`** and **`reconciliation_cadence`** (Configuration), which make the liveness arm a bounded claim rather than an "eventually". This is the invariant the formal model checks (the Ledger's `formal:` line).

- **Invariant 4 — Downstream registration is recorded.** Every binding in `consent_to_downstream[consent_id]` has a corresponding `processing.registered` Audit Trail event carrying its `processing_scope`, `processor_ref`, and `consent_id`; a binding is added to the set (step 5) only after its event lands (step 4). *Rests on:* [Register Processing]'s audit-first ordering and Audit Trail attribution. This is what makes the `affected_scopes` set on a propagation event *itself* records-grounded — every scope it enumerates traces to a recorded registration act, so the propagation set is not a free-floating assertion. *Scope, stated honestly:* the invariant grounds every **registered** binding; it does not and cannot assert that every real-world processing activity was registered — that outward completeness is the deployment's registration obligation (Edge cases — *Registration completeness is a deployment obligation*), the registration mirror of Invariant 1(b)'s gate obligation.

- **Invariant 5 — Records-alone consent lifecycle.** The complete lifecycle of every consent governed by this composition — grant (`consent.grant_intended`, then `consent.granted` + the Consent record), downstream registrations (`processing.registered` events + `consent_to_downstream`), withdrawal (`consent.withdrawal_intended`, then `consent.revoked` with `affected_scopes` + the Consent Revoked record), and any history access (`consent.history-read`) — is reconstructable from the Consent store, the Audit Trail (Event Log reached transitively), and the composition maps, without recourse to source code or developer narration. *Rests on:* Invariants 2–4, Consent's store durability (Consent Invariant 8 — terminal records retained), and Audit Trail's forensic completability.

- **Invariant 6 — Consent-record retention floor.** Every consent record governed by this composition (in any state, including Revoked or Expired) remains in the Consent store, and its consent-record Retention Window record remains `Retained`. *Rests on:* Consent Invariant 8 (no consent record is removed from the store — stated by the atom without qualification, and this composition adds none) plus Retention Window Invariant 7 (no early purge). The two constituents say two different things about the same record, and both hold: Retention Window's Invariant 7 is the structural floor — nothing can destroy the record before `retention_until` even in a deployment that wires `purge` — and Consent's Invariant 8 is what holds past the floor. Because Retention Window defines `Purged` as the state of a retention whose underlying record has been destroyed, a consent-record retention **never lawfully reaches `Purged`**; when the proof-period obligation elapses, the observable fact is Retention Window's read-time `Purge Eligible` projection turning true, which is a derived value and not a transition. `consent_to_retention[consent_id]` is the records-alone evidence the placement occurred. A withdrawn consent must outlive its own withdrawal: it is the proof, under GDPR Article 7(1), that the controller once had a valid basis and lawfully ended it.

- **Invariant 7 — Inward/outward authorization separation.** No consent-administration action ([Record Consent], [Register Processing], [Withdraw Consent], [Read Consent History]) proceeds without a passing Permissions check for its scope; and the [Processing Permitted] gate consults **only** Consent state, never Permissions. *Defended in-line:* each administration action's step 1 is a `Permissions.permitted` check; [Processing Permitted] calls only `Consent.check`. *Why load-bearing:* Consent is an *outward* authorization (the data subject authorizes the system to process their data); Permissions is an *inward* authorization (the organization authorizes an operator to administer records). Collapsing them — gating processing on an operator's permission, or gating administration on the subject's consent — is the exact error Consent's Intent warns against. Keeping the two surfaces distinct is what lets an auditor answer *"was this operator authorized to record this withdrawal?"* (Permissions) and *"did this subject consent to this processing?"* (Consent) as separate, separately-evidenced questions.

- **Invariant 8 — Authentication precedes commitment at the four administration actions.** At [Record Consent], [Register Processing], [Withdraw Consent] and [Read Consent History] — the actions that accept a `credential` and act on a named operator's authority — no `Consent` or `Retention Window` call that commits **on the caller's authority**, and no composition map write **recording the invocation's own effect**, is reached on any path before that operator's credential has validated against the actor registry's public material for the supplied `actor_ref`. Two things are outside that quantifier and are named rather than left to inference. A **derived-index rebuild** — which [Withdraw Consent] step 4 explicitly authorizes where the index's integrity is in doubt — reconstructs from committed audit events, carries no caller input, and records no effect of this invocation. The **`Expired` stored-state transition** a lazily-implemented Consent instance writes inside `read` (Consent Invariant 6) materializes a value already true by the record's own stored deadline and likewise relies on no actor's authority. Both are the rule's third answer, not exemptions from it. The quantifier is scoped to those four deliberately: [Processing Permitted] accepts no credential, names no actor and commits nothing, so it relies on no actor's authority and has nothing to authenticate. Two mechanisms discharge the invariant, and which applies is a property of where each action's first `record_action` sits. At [Register Processing] and [Read Consent History] **nothing commits that relies on the caller's authority before the audit write** — at [Register Processing] the audit write *is* the first committing call, the binding following it; at [Read Consent History] the only write reachable ahead of it is the `Expired` stored-state transition a lazily-implemented Consent instance must perform inside `read` (Consent Invariant 6), which materializes a value already true by the record's own deadline, carries no caller content, and relies on no actor's authority — the rule's third answer. Neither action needs an additional mechanism. At [Record Consent] and [Withdraw Consent] the first committing call is a `Consent` call, so each opens with an **intent record** whose `record_action` performs the validation before that call; `invalid-credential` is a pre-state refusal with nothing committed, and the outcome event carries `intent_event_id` back for an exact per-invocation join.

  **What this establishes for Invariant 7, and what it does not.** Invariant 7 asserts that every administration action was authorized; `Permissions.permitted` takes an `actor_ref` and no credential, so before this discipline that assertion ran over an identity nobody had verified — a caller who knew a privileged `actor_ref` satisfied it. The intent record does not move or replace the Permissions check; it establishes that the caller **is** the actor whose authority the check consulted, before anything commits, which is what makes Invariant 7's claim about an operator a claim about a *verified* operator. It establishes nothing about the data subject: `subject_ref` is never authenticated here, and whether the subject's consent was freely given and informed stays an externally-clearable check. *Rests on:* Audit Trail's `record_action` and the Actor Identity attestation reached transitively through it. *Defended in-line:* the ordering is visible in all four actions' step lists, and Generation acceptance check 6 tests it from the records alone.

Verification of the consent gate (Invariant 1) plus grant audit coverage (Invariant 2) gives the *substantiated-processing* property — every consent-based action was gated on a recorded, attributed consent. Revocation propagation completeness (Invariant 3) plus downstream-registration recording (Invariant 4) gives the *propagated-withdrawal* property — every withdrawal's downstream impact is completely enumerated and itself records-grounded. Records-alone lifecycle (Invariant 5) and the retention floor (Invariant 6) close the consent lifecycle: the full history survives and is reconstructable. Invariant 7 keeps the two authorization surfaces distinct, so neither masquerades as the other, and Invariant 8 is what makes Invariant 7's inward half a claim about a verified operator rather than an asserted one.

## Examples

### Walkthrough — marketing-consent management under GDPR

A retail platform uses this composition to manage marketing consent. Configuration: `consent_record_retention_policy_ref = gdpr_consent_proof_6yr`, `audit_trail_retention_policy = gdpr_audit_10yr`. Permission grants: the consent-service actor holds `consent:grant`, `consent:register-processing`, `consent:revoke`, `consent:read`.

1. **Record consent.** A user affirms a marketing-email consent banner; the consent service calls `record_consent(actor_ref="consent_svc", subject_ref="user-4491", purpose="marketing:email", credential=<consent_svc>, retention_policy_ref="gdpr_consent_proof_6yr", expires_at="2027-05-13T00:00:00Z") → consent_id = cns-7001`. Internally: `Permissions.permitted("consent_svc", "consent:grant") → permitted`; then — **before anything commits** — Audit Trail records `consent.grant_intended` → `ev_grant_int_01`, which is where the consent service's credential is checked against the actor registry (had it not validated, the call would have returned `rejected(invalid-credential)` with no consent granted and no retention placed); then `Consent.grant(...) → cns-7001` (Granted); `Retention Window.place_under_retention(cns-7001, gdpr_consent_proof_6yr) → ret_cns_7001`; Audit Trail records `consent.granted` carrying `intent_event_id: ev_grant_int_01`; maps populated. Note what the intent event does **not** carry: no `cns-7001`, which did not exist when it was written and which only `consent.granted` may make resolvable.

2. **Register downstream processing.** Two systems will rely on this consent. `register_processing(actor_ref="consent_svc", consent_id="cns-7001", processing_scope="email-campaign-engine", processor_ref="campaigns@platform", credential=<consent_svc>) → registered`, and again for `processing_scope="lookalike-audience-builder", processor_ref="adtech@platform"`. Each records a `processing.registered` event; `consent_to_downstream[cns-7001]` now holds two bindings.

3. **Gate check before processing.** Before sending a campaign, the email engine calls `processing_permitted("user-4491", "marketing:email") → permitted`. The campaign proceeds.

4. **Withdrawal with propagation.** The user clicks "unsubscribe / withdraw consent." The consent service calls `withdraw_consent(actor_ref="consent_svc", consent_id="cns-7001", credential=<consent_svc>, reason="user-withdrawal-via-preferences") → withdrawn`. Internally: `Permissions.permitted("consent_svc", "consent:revoke") → permitted`; `affected_scopes` computed from `consent_to_downstream[cns-7001]` = `{email-campaign-engine@campaigns, lookalike-audience-builder@adtech}`; Audit Trail records `consent.withdrawal_intended` → `ev_wd_int_01`, verifying the credential before the terminal transition and carrying the `consent_id` but **not** the `affected_scopes` (one authoritative carrier, Invariant 3); `Consent.revoke(cns-7001, ...) → revoked`; Audit Trail records `consent.revoked` carrying `intent_event_id: ev_wd_int_01` and the complete `affected_scopes` set — *atomically with the revoke*. Invariant 3 holds: the withdrawal and its complete downstream enumeration are one sealed act. Invariant 8 holds and is checkable: `ev_wd_int_01` precedes the propagation event in the Event Log's order (their `intended_at` and `revoked_at` are the same injected instant, so the `sequence_number` is what settles it) and names the same operator.

5. **Gate check after withdrawal.** `processing_permitted("user-4491", "marketing:email") → rejected(not-permitted(revoked))`. The email engine and the audience builder, consuming the propagation event or re-querying the gate, cease processing; deleting the derived lookalike audience is the audience builder's own obligation, signalled by the `consent.revoked` event's `affected_scopes`.

6. **DSAR audit.** A data-subject access request calls `read_consent_history(actor_ref="dsr_officer", subject_ref="user-4491", credential=<dsr_officer>)` → the Granted-then-Revoked `cns-7001` record; a `consent.history-read` meta-event is recorded. The complete lifecycle — the grant and its intent, two registrations, the withdrawal-with-propagation and its intent, the access — is reconstructable from the records (Invariant 5), and each state-changing act's records show the operator was verified before it committed (Invariant 8).

### Re-consent path

Six months after withdrawal the user re-enables marketing email. `record_consent(...) → cns-7042` (a fresh Granted record; `cns-7001` remains Revoked as evidence). `processing_permitted("user-4491", "marketing:email") → permitted` (Consent's `check` evaluates the most-recent grant). Downstream processing is re-registered against the new `consent_id`; the prior consent's propagation record is untouched history.

### Rejection path — withdrawal by an unauthorized operator

An operator lacking `consent:revoke` attempts `withdraw_consent(...)`: step 1's `Permissions.permitted → denied` → `rejected(permission-denied)`. No consent state changes; no propagation event is recorded. (Invariant 7 — administration is permission-gated.)

### Rejection path — withdrawing an already-revoked consent

A retry after a timeout: `withdraw_consent(actor_ref="consent_svc", consent_id="cns-7001", credential=<consent_svc>, reason="retry")` → the step-5 intent event is written (the operator is real and their credential validates), then step 6 maps Consent's `already-revoked` → `rejected(already-revoked)`. No second propagation event is recorded; the single `consent.revoked` event from the first withdrawal stands. The retry therefore leaves a `consent.withdrawal_intended` event with no `consent.revoked` event carrying its `intent_event_id` — the expected residue of the authentication discipline, and the worked instance of case (ii) in Generation acceptance check 6's triage: the consent is Revoked, but by *another* invocation's propagation event, so nothing is owed and no compensating record may be written. (Consent Invariant 3 — terminal absorption — surfaced at the composition boundary.)

### Failure path — withdrawal whose propagation event fails to record

A [Withdraw Consent] call passes steps 1–5, revokes at step 6, and step 7's `record_action` fails (`storage-failure` in the substrate): the call returns `rejected(recording-failure)` with the consent Revoked in Consent but the propagation unattested — the composition's most consequential atomicity gap where the host transaction boundary was unavailable. The implementation recovers per the `(step)`-aware discipline — a substrate `storage-failure` at the append means no event landed, so the pre-check finds none and the `record_action` is retried until it lands (a step-4 retention-arm failure would instead leave the event appended, and the pre-check would find it and re-append nothing) — and surfaces the Revoked-but-unpropagated consent as a high-priority compliance finding meanwhile; the compensating `consent.revoked` event, when it lands, is attested under the composition's service identity behind a `consent.recovery_intended` record, carries `cascade_recovery = true`, names the operator as `revoked_by`, and points at the original `consent.withdrawal_intended` event as its `intent_event_id`, so an auditor distinguishes the recovered withdrawal from a clean one and still reads who withdrew (Edge cases — *Cross-store consistency under partial failure*). The gate is already honest during the gap: `processing_permitted` reads Consent, so it returns `rejected(not-permitted(revoked))` from the moment step 6 committed.

### Rejection path — gate query for a subject who never consented

`processing_permitted("user-9999", "marketing:email")` where no consent record exists → `Consent.check → not-known` → `rejected(not-permitted(not-known))`. The processing system distinguishes *never consented* from *withdrawn* and requests consent rather than honoring a withdrawal that never happened.

## Regulated adversarial scenarios

**Regulator audit — "prove every consent-based processing activity was gated on valid consent, and that withdrawals propagated."** A data protection authority examines the Audit Trail and the processing records. For every downstream processing activity *the deployment discloses* (the composition's records enumerate the registered set; whether that set is outwardly complete is the deployment's registration obligation, verified against the deployment's own processing inventory — an externally-clearable question), the examiner confirms a `processing.registered` event bound it to a `consent_id` (Invariant 4); for the consent governing it, the examiner confirms a `consent.granted` event (Invariant 2) and that `AuditTrail.verify_record` returns `verified` for both. For every withdrawn consent, the examiner confirms exactly one `consent.revoked` event whose `affected_scopes` enumerates every `processing.registered` binding for that `consent_id` (Invariant 3) — a withdrawal whose `affected_scopes` omits a registered binding, or a revoked Consent record with no `consent.revoked` event at all, is a conformance failure. The examiner consults no source code or runbooks; the gate is the single processing precondition (Invariant 1) and the propagation event is the single record of downstream impact.

**Disputed withdrawal — data subject claims processing continued after they withdrew consent.** A data subject complains that marketing continued after withdrawal. The investigator retrieves the `consent.revoked` event for the subject's consent: it carries `revoked_at`, `reason`, and the complete `affected_scopes` set naming `email-campaign-engine@campaigns` and `lookalike-audience-builder@adtech`. The investigator can therefore name *exactly which processors were on notice* and when. Consent Invariant 9 (revocation non-retroactivity) and the Audit Trail seal establish the withdrawal record was not altered or back-dated. If a named processor continued after `revoked_at`, that is that processor's compliance failure — this composition's records prove the withdrawal was recorded, propagated, and enumerated; whether each named processor *acted* on it is the processor's obligation, signalled but not performed by this composition. The propagation event is the rebuttal to "we were never told."

**Breach or incident forensics — which subjects had withdrawn consent during the compromise window, and was any withdrawal record altered.** An incident investigator is given a window and must reconstruct which consents were Granted versus Revoked during it and whether any withdrawal or its propagation set was tampered with. The investigator replays each consent's `consent.granted`, `processing.registered`, and `consent.revoked` events in Event Log insertion order (reached transitively through Audit Trail), determining each consent's state and downstream-binding set at the window's bounds, then runs `AuditTrail.verify_record` against representative events in each seal's coverage to bound any tampering window. Invariant 3 is load-bearing: a `consent.revoked` event whose `affected_scopes` was silently shrunk to hide a processor that should have stopped is foreclosed by the append-only, sealed log — the propagation set is part of the hashed event payload, so an alteration breaks the seal. The newest events in the Audit Trail's unsealed tail carry Event Log per-event immutability but become seal-verifiable only after the next seal cadence; until then a tail event returns `failed-verification(unsealed)`, so the integrity bound on the most recent withdrawals is the substrate's unsealed-tail policy (a tighter `seal_cadence` narrows it).

## Generation acceptance

A derived implementation of this composition is *acceptable* — in the regulator-acceptance sense — when an external auditor, given the composition's emergent state plus the Consent, Permissions, Retention Window, and Audit Trail substrate stores, can do all of the following without recourse to source code, runbooks, or developer narration.

### Audit-Trail-traversal-clearable checks

1. **Grant coverage.** For every consent reachable through this composition in any state, there exists a `consent.granted` Audit Trail event carrying its `consent_id`, for which `AuditTrail.verify_record` returns `verified`. A consent governed by this composition with no `consent.granted` event is a conformance failure (Invariant 2). **Symmetric reading:** every record in the Consent store instance has a `consent.granted` event — a store record with *no* event is, **at quiescence**, the direct-write bypass signal against the exclusive-ownership obligation (*Composes*), a consent that can pass the gate but can never be withdrawn with propagation through this composition. During a [Record Consent] step-6 recovery window the same signature is a pending-compensation orphan, already surfaced as a finding; the two are distinguished by resolution — the orphan gains its event (or a compensation) when recovery lands, a bypass never does. **The symmetric reading runs between two edges** (Configuration, `administration_completion_bound`): a store record whose `granted_at` is younger than the bound may belong to an invocation still between `Consent.grant` and its `consent.granted` write and is inconclusive, not a signature; one whose `granted_at` lies past `audit_trail_retention_policy`'s horizon has had its grant event lawfully destroyed, and its absence is destruction, never a bypass — Composition state's *Past the horizon* paragraph is what still answers for it.

2. **Propagation completeness.** For every Revoked consent (per the Consent store) governed by this composition, there exists exactly one `consent.revoked` event whose `affected_scopes` set equals the set of `processing.registered` bindings for that `consent_id` ordered before **that withdrawal's `consent.withdrawal_intended` event** — the event the propagation event names as its `intent_event_id` — and `AuditTrail.verify_record` returns `verified` for it. A Revoked consent with more than one `consent.revoked` event, or one whose `affected_scopes` omits a binding registered before that boundary, is a conformance failure (Invariant 3).

  **The boundary is the intent event and not the propagation event, and the difference is not pedantic.** The obvious reading — *registered before the `consent.revoked` event in the log* — is sound for a clean withdrawal, because the *Concurrency* edge case makes per-`consent_id` serialization of [Register Processing] and [Withdraw Consent] a hard conformance requirement, so nothing can be registered between the step-4 read and the step-7 append of one invocation. It is **not** sound for a compensated one. [Register Processing] is deliberately permitted against an already-Revoked consent, and the serialization obligation binds the two *invocations*, not the span from an original step-4 read to a compensating append made in a later invocation. A registration landing inside a recovery window therefore sits before the compensating event and outside its correctly-computed set, and the old boundary would have branded a correct compensation a conformance failure. Anchoring to the intent event — which is inside the serialized invocation that did the read — fixes the point for clean and compensated withdrawals alike, and is what makes the set **re-derivable** by a compensation rather than lost with the process that computed it.

  **A Revoked consent with *no* `consent.revoked` event is not automatically a failure**, because Invariant 3 admits it as a reachable partial; it is a failure only if it is unsurfaced or uncompensated, which check 7 (*Unpropagated revocations are surfaced and compensated*) tests, and only after the five-case triage of check 6 — in particular case (v), which forbids reading a direct-write bypass as this composition's orphan.

3. **Registration grounding.** For every `processing_scope`/`processor_ref` pair appearing in any `consent.revoked` event's `affected_scopes`, there exists a `processing.registered` event with the same `consent_id` ordered before that withdrawal's `consent.withdrawal_intended` event — the same boundary check 2 uses, and for the same reason. An `affected_scope` with no precipitating registration event, or one whose registration lands after the boundary, is a conformance failure (Invariant 4).

4. **Gate-result consistency.** For a sample of `(subject_ref, purpose)` pairs, [Processing Permitted] returns `permitted` exactly when `Consent.check` returns `granted` (Invariant 1). The auditor re-runs both and confirms agreement.

5. **Consent-record retention (existence).** For every consent governed by this composition, the Retention Window store contains a consent-record retention (the `consent_record_retention_id` in `consent_to_retention`) — in `Retained` state — unconditionally, including where `retention_until` has elapsed and `Purge Eligible` reads true. What the check forbids is an absent placement or a consent-record retention in `Purged`: by Retention Window's own definition that state records the destruction of the underlying record, which Consent's Invariant 8 forbids and check 7 independently detects through Consent's no-destruction check (Invariant 6). A deployment that reads `Purge Eligible` on this instance as permission to invoke `purge` fails both. (Whether the configured policy's duration *meets the applicable regulatory proof-period minimum* is an externally-clearable check.)

6. **Authentication precedence at the administration actions.** For every `consent.granted` and `consent.revoked` event, take its `data.intent_event_id`, resolve it to the matching intent event (`consent.grant_intended` / `consent.withdrawal_intended`), and confirm that event precedes it in the Event Log's own order and names the same operator — the same `actor_ref` for an event the invocation wrote itself; for a compensating event (`cascade_recovery = true`) the `actor_ref` is the composition's `application_actor_ref` and the operator is its `data.granted_by` / `data.revoked_by`, which must equal the intent event's `actor_ref`, and a `consent.recovery_intended` record naming the same `intent_event_id` must precede the compensating event. Because the substrate validates the caller's credential against the actor registry inside every `record_action`, the named intent event *is* the records-alone proof that the operator was authenticated before the consent was granted or revoked — which is what makes Invariant 8 verifiable rather than asserted, and what makes Invariant 7's authorization claim a claim about a verified operator. **The order must be read from the `sequence_number`, never from the timestamps.** For a withdrawal the two events carry the same injected instant by construction, so the stamps cannot separate them; for a grant the outcome event carries no stamp at all and the Consent record's `granted_at` is taken at the atom's own seam, so the stamps are not comparable in the first place (*Logic confinement*). The `sequence_number` is the only order source that spans both events. **The join must be by `intent_event_id`**, not by `{actor_ref, subject_ref, purpose}` — one operator granting the same subject the same purpose repeatedly is ordinary use (the re-consent path is a worked instance), so a single stale intent event would satisfy this check for an unbounded number of later grants. [Register Processing] and [Read Consent History] carry no intent event and need none: their audit write is itself the first committing call, so the ordering is structural and there is nothing to join.

  **An intent event with no outcome event is not a failure**, and the two kinds triage differently because they carry different keys. A `consent.withdrawal_intended` event carries the `consent_id`, so the triage reads that consent's state — joining by `intent_event_id`, never by `consent_id`, since two invocations can name one consent and only one of them can have done the revoking. **(i)** The consent is Revoked and **no** `consent.revoked` event names that `consent_id` at all: the revocation committed and its propagation event was lost — this is the Revoked-but-unpropagated orphan the *Cross-store consistency* edge case already owns, recovered by its own `(step)`-aware discipline, and the compensating event carries `cascade_recovery = true`. **(ii)** The consent is Revoked and a `consent.revoked` event names it carrying a *different* `intent_event_id`: another invocation revoked it and this one was refused `already-revoked` — nothing is owed, and compensating here would write a second propagation event for one withdrawal, breaking Invariant 3's exactly-one. **(iii)** The consent exists and is not Revoked: `Consent.revoke` never landed (`already-expired`, or a `storage-failure`, or a death before the call) — nothing is owed. **(iv)** The consent resolves in `consent_to_subject_purpose` but the Consent store does not know it: that is the internal-consistency anomaly step 6 already names and is a finding in its own right, not a compensation case. **(v)** **Before applying case (i) at all**, confirm a `consent.withdrawal_intended` event exists naming that `consent_id`. A direct `Consent.revoke` against the shared instance — the write-ownership bypass — presents in exactly case (i)'s shape: Revoked, with a `consent.granted` event and no `consent.revoked` event. A bypass never entered this composition, so it can have left no withdrawal-intent event, while an orphan of this composition's own step 7 always has one. Where none exists, **no compensation is owed and none may be written** — writing one would manufacture a propagation record for a withdrawal this composition never performed and attribute it to an operator who never called; report a write-ownership bypass instead. Two ways to get this wrong, both of which write a false record: an auditor who stops at *is it revoked?* will misclassify **(ii)** as **(i)**, and one who never asks case **(v)** will compensate a bypass.

  A `consent.grant_intended` event carries **no `consent_id`**, because none existed when it was written, so it cannot be joined to a consent at all. What it does is narrow a distinction check 1 could previously make only by waiting. Check 1's symmetric reading finds Consent store records with no `consent.granted` event and says they are either a pending step-6 orphan or a direct-write bypass, *distinguished by resolution*. (That disjunction is not exhaustive — a grant refused at step 5 leaves a Granted, unretained record in the same shape, and check 1 does not name it; that gap is a finding of its own, carried open.) A bypass never entered this composition, so it can have no intent event; an orphan of this composition's own step 6 will have one, from the same `actor_ref`, in the same window. That is a **signal available immediately**, not a proof, and an auditor must expect surplus intent events on the grant side as the normal case rather than the exception. The largest source is not concurrency but ordinary refusal: every [Record Consent] refused at step 4 or step 5 leaves a `consent.grant_intended` event with no store record and no orphan behind it at all. Concurrent grants by one actor add more. The correspondence is by actor and window rather than by identity, so the signal narrows the disjunction and never closes it. Use it to triage first and to wait second; do not report a bypass on its strength alone.

7. **Unpropagated revocations are surfaced and compensated, and the compensation is bounded.** Invariant 3 admits the Revoked-but-unpropagated consent as a reachable partial, so the auditable question is not whether one exists but whether it was seen and repaired. For every Revoked consent with no `consent.revoked` event, first run check 6's five-case triage — **case (v) is a precondition, not a formality**: absent a `consent.withdrawal_intended` event naming that `consent_id`, the consent was revoked by a direct-write bypass, nothing is owed, and compensating would manufacture a propagation record for a withdrawal this composition never performed. Where the triage lands on case (i), confirm **either** a `consent.revoked` event carrying `cascade_recovery = true` that landed within `compensation_window` of the consent's `revoked_at` — attested under `application_actor_ref`, preceded by its `consent.recovery_intended` record, naming the operator as `revoked_by`, and carrying as `intent_event_id` the `consent.withdrawal_intended` event whose `intended_at` equals the consent's `revoked_at` and whose `actor_ref` equals its `revoked_by` (the earliest such event by `sequence_number` where the clock's resolution admits more than one; where the pairing is still undecidable the compensating event carries `intent_event_candidates` naming every match, and an auditor reads the set, not a guess) — **or** an open compliance finding naming that `consent_id`. The check runs between the same two edges as check 1: a Revoked consent whose `revoked_at` is younger than `administration_completion_bound` is inconclusive (its invocation may be about to write), and one whose `revoked_at` is past the audit horizon has had its propagation event lawfully destroyed — reported as *propagation history purged*, never as an unpropagated revocation and never as a bypass, since the `consent.withdrawal_intended` event case (v) would look for is destroyed with it. Neither is a conformance failure; *neither present* is — the orphan was silent, which is what the second safety arm forbids. Two further reads make the check real. **The compensating event's `affected_scopes` must equal the set derived at the intent-event boundary**, not at the compensating event's own position — check 2's boundary rule is what makes that testable, and it is the whole reason a compensation can be verified rather than merely accepted. **And `compensation_window` and `reconciliation_cadence` must both be declared** (Configuration), with the cadence no longer than the window: an undeclared window makes the liveness arm unfalsifiable *and leaves the retry discipline with no terminus*, so a permanent failure presents as an open loop rather than an escalation, and a cadence longer than the window makes the window unmeetable. Either way the invariant is deferred rather than held, and an auditor should see that from the configuration alone.

8. **The audit horizon outlasts the consents it records.** Compare the horizon of `audit_trail_retention_policy` against the consent-record retention duration in use (`consent_record_retention_policy_ref`). **The audit instance's horizon must be the longer.** This is not a defensibility preference: three of the four emergent maps have the audit traversal as their **only** permitted rebuild source, the constituent-store fallback being forbidden so that a traversal/enumeration disagreement can detect a write-ownership bypass (Composition state). A violation is therefore a **conformance failure**, and the failure it admits is the live one — a `consent.granted` payload destroyed while its consent is still Granted leaves `consent_to_subject_purpose` unrebuildable and [Withdraw Consent] answering `not-known` to a valid withdrawal, contrary to GDPR Article 7(3). Confirm also that the implementation does **not** resolve a `consent_id` from the Consent store when the traversal misses: that is the forbidden fallback, and taking it would trade an availability failure for a security one by making a bypassed `consent_id` resolvable.

  **Two limits of this check, stated so neither is read as something else.** It cannot be run at all for consents whose grant events have already aged out — their absence is indistinguishable from a bypass, which is the detector's own convergence past the horizon. And where the consent-record retention duration is a host-declared value behind a `policy_ref` this composition does not resolve, the comparison itself is externally-clearable and is listed there; what stays checkable here is the absence of the forbidden fallback.

  **Check 2's coverage is bounded by the same horizon**, and that bound is a limit of the check rather than a defect in any fulfillment: verifying a propagation event's `affected_scopes` against the registrations before its intent event requires those `processing.registered` payloads, and past the horizon the verification is unavailable. An auditor reports it as unverifiable, never as a coverage failure.

9. **Constituent Generation acceptance bars.** Verify each constituent's own Generation acceptance bar over its store: Consent's seven checks, Permissions' five checks, Retention Window's six checks, Audit Trail's eight checks.

### Externally-clearable checks

These audit questions arise around this composition but cannot be answered from the composition's records alone:

- **Whether the registered set is complete.** The composition's records enumerate every *registered* processing activity; whether every real-world consent-based processing activity was in fact registered requires the deployment's own processing inventory (a records-of-processing-activities register under GDPR Article 30) compared against the `processing.registered` events — the registration-completeness deployment obligation (Edge cases).
- **Whether a named downstream processor actually ceased processing after a withdrawal.** This composition records that a withdrawal was propagated and which scopes it named; it cannot prove that `lookalike-audience-builder` deleted its derived audience. That assurance requires the processor's own records — the propagation event is notice, not enforcement.
- **Whether the consent was freely given, specific, and informed.** This composition records the grant, its `granted_by`, `purpose`, and (via `metadata`) the consent-form version; the GDPR Article 7 quality of the consent (was the banner non-deceptive, was consent unbundled) is a UX (user experience)-and-legal question the records cannot settle alone.
- **Whether the operator who recorded a consent action was authorized under organizational policy.** This composition enforces a Permissions check and binds the operator cryptographically via the Audit Trail's Actor Identity, but whether the *grant of that permission* reflected correct organizational authority is governed by the Permissions-administration layer (a composing peer — Attributed Permissions Admin).
- **Whether the consent-record retention duration meets the regulatory proof-period floor.** This composition records that a consent-record retention exists and its `retention_until`; verifying that the configured `consent_record_retention_policy_ref` encodes at least the applicable minimum requires the deployment's Retention Window policy registry and knowledge of the regulatory floor — a Configuration deployment obligation.
- **Whether processing rested on consent at all.** This composition governs the consent basis (GDPR Article 6(1)(a)); whether a given processing activity should have rested on a *different* lawful basis (legitimate interest, legal obligation) is a basis-selection question outside this composition — a Customer Due Diligence obligation under Customer Onboarding rests on Article 6(1)(c), not consent, and does not fall within this composition's scope.

## Edge cases and explicit non-goals

- **Cross-store consistency under partial failure.** Several of this composition's actions write to two or three stores in sequence; a failure between writes leaves partial state. The most consequential gap is in [Withdraw Consent]: step 6 revokes in Consent, then step 7 records the `consent.revoked` propagation event; if step 7 fails, the consent is Revoked but the withdrawal is **unpropagated** — a Revoked consent with no `consent.revoked` event, violating Invariant 3. Consent's revocation is immutable once committed, so synchronous rollback is not available. **There is no host transaction over steps 6–7 and there cannot be, and an earlier revision of this entry prescribed one.** It said the conforming implementation commits the revoke and the propagation event within one host transaction boundary, and treated the orphan as arising *"where the host cannot provide that atomicity"* — a conditional that reads as a deployment-specific caveat and is in fact the only case. This very entry states why, one sentence earlier: Consent's revocation is immutable once committed. The substrate's append is the same. **Two irreversible writes cannot be enlisted in a transaction, so what was described as the exception was the whole of the behavior**, and the prescription named a mechanism no deployment could supply. What the pair actually has is an **ordering** — revoke, then append — which is what makes this partial the recoverable one and its mirror unreachable (Invariant 3). The step-5 intent record stands before both, since it is the write that verified the revoker and must precede the revocation it authorizes, and it additionally fixes the propagation set's boundary (Generation acceptance check 2). Accordingly, the implementation must (a) recover **`(step)`-aware and pre-checked**: first traverse the log for an existing `consent.revoked` event carrying this `consent_id` — the substrate's `recording-failure(step)` step-4 arm (retention placement over the audit event) leaves the event already appended and attested, where a blind retry would append a second `consent.revoked` against Invariant 3's exactly-one and check 2 (that arm's gap belongs to the substrate's own Invariant-2 reconciliation, and this composition treats the event as landed); only where no event landed is the `record_action` retried until it lands — and (b) immediately surface the orphan (a Revoked consent with no propagation event) to the compliance dashboard as a high-priority finding, with the compensating `consent.revoked` event, once it lands, carrying a `cascade_recovery = true` marker so an auditor can distinguish a clean withdrawal from a recovered one.

  **The retry has a terminus, and it needed one.** *Until it lands* is not a discipline for a permanent cause — a substrate that will never accept the append, or an `affected_scopes` payload that exceeds the substrate's cap — and a loop with no bound is indistinguishable, from the records, from an orphan nobody is working on. An orphan not compensated within the declared `compensation_window` (Configuration) **escalates as an unresolved compliance finding** rather than remaining an open retry. For the payload case specifically: `consent_to_downstream` is never pruned, so a long-lived consent's set grows without bound, and the propagation event may carry a **cryptographic digest of the set together with its cardinality** in place of the enumeration — mechanism-neutral, exactly as Immutable Transaction Ledger treats its subset proofs, and verifiable here in a way it is not everywhere, because the set is re-derivable from the `processing.registered` events at the recorded boundary rather than existing only in the payload.

  **The compensation re-derives the set; it does not re-read it.** Re-reading `consent_to_downstream` at compensation time would be wrong, because [Register Processing] is deliberately permitted against an already-Revoked consent, so the index can have grown since the withdrawal — and a compensating event enumerating a scope registered *after* the subject withdrew would misstate what the withdrawal covered. The set is instead derived from the `processing.registered` events ordered before that withdrawal's `consent.withdrawal_intended` event, which is the boundary Generation acceptance check 2 defines and the reason the intent event is load-bearing for Invariant 3 and not only for Invariant 8. *(That derivation reads event payloads, and so inherits the retention-horizon bound on any payload-sourced rebuild — the `Q-rebuild-bound` class, routed separately on this pattern and named here rather than solved.)*

  **The reconciliation scan is mandatory, not an implementation nicety.** The orphan is reachable by a *return* (the action rejects and surfaces it in its own outcome) and by a *crash* (nothing returns and nothing is surfaced). Only the first surfaces itself; the second is what Invariant 3's second safety arm is actually about. A conforming deployment runs the scan **at process restart** and on the `reconciliation_cadence` interval (Configuration): enumerate Consent store records in the Revoked state **whose `revoked_at` lies between the two edges** — older than `administration_completion_bound`, so the invocation that revoked is not still about to write its own propagation event, and inside `audit_trail_retention_policy`'s horizon, so the event's absence can be a gap rather than lawful destruction (a record past the horizon is reported *propagation history purged* and is not triaged, since case (v)'s own evidence is destroyed with it) — take those with no `consent.revoked` event naming them, and put each through Generation acceptance check 6's five-case triage — which already distinguishes the compensable orphan (i) from the already-propagated (ii), the never-revoked (iii), the index anomaly (iv), and, in case (v), the direct-write bypass that must **not** be compensated. The scan runs under the same per-`consent_id` serialization as the actions (*Concurrency*), so a compensation and an invocation never interleave on one consent. The scan is the machinery that discipline was written for and previously had no scheduled caller.

  **What the scan writes, and as whom.** The operator who withdrew is not present when the scan runs, so nothing the scan writes may be attested as that operator. Every compensating write is made under the composition's **service identity** (`application_actor_ref` / `application_credential`, Configuration), and each is preceded by its own **`consent.recovery_intended`** record — `data={kind: propagation, consent_id, intent_event_id, revoked_at}` — naming the invocation it repairs and what it is about to append, so the composition's own trail shows a compensation was occasioned by the scan and not by a direct call. The compensating `consent.revoked` event then carries `cascade_recovery = true`, the operator as `revoked_by`, and `intent_event_id` = the original `consent.withdrawal_intended` event. **That pairing is by the records, not by guess:** the revoking invocation passed its injected `now` to `Consent.revoke` as `revoked_at` and stamped the same reading as the intent's `intended_at` (*Logic confinement*), and it named the operator as `revoked_by`, so the intent to pair with is the `consent.withdrawal_intended` event for this `consent_id` whose `intended_at` equals the record's `revoked_at` and whose `actor_ref` equals its `revoked_by` — the earliest by `sequence_number` where the deployment's clock resolution admits more than one, since under serialization a refused retry's intent can only follow the revoking one. Where the pairing is still undecidable the compensating event carries **`intent_event_candidates`** naming every match rather than choosing, and its `affected_scopes` is derived at the earliest candidate's boundary. Everything else the event carries is re-derived — `subject_ref` and `purpose` from `consent_to_subject_purpose`, `reason` and `revoked_at` from the Consent record, `affected_scopes` from the `processing.registered` events before the boundary — and nothing is remembered from the process that died.

  **The [Record Consent] side has two orphan shapes, and both are named here with their resolution.** *Shape A — granted and retained, no event* (a step-6 failure): the store holds a Granted record and the consent-record Retention Window instance holds its placement. *Shape B — granted and unretained, no event* (a step-5 failure): the store holds the record, no placement exists, and no `consent_record_retention_id` can ever appear on a grant event until one does. The scan enumerates Consent store records with no `consent.granted` event naming them, between the same two edges (`granted_at` older than the bound, inside the horizon), and for each first asks the grant-side question that mirrors case (v): is there a `consent.grant_intended` event, inside the bound before the record's `granted_at`, from the actor the record names as `granted_by`, carrying the record's `subject_ref` and `purpose`? None → a direct-write bypass; nothing is owed and nothing may be written; report it. Exactly one → the orphan's intent. More than one (the same operator granting the same subject the same purpose twice inside one bound) → the candidates are named, never chosen. Then the resolution, under the service identity behind a `consent.recovery_intended` record (`kind: grant`, the `consent_id`, the intent or candidates, and the plan): for shape B, **re-run** step 5 — `Retention Window.place_under_retention(record_ref=consent_id, policy_ref=<the intent event's retention_policy_ref>)`, the policy taken from the intent payload the operator wrote, which is why the intent carries it — and only then the event; for both shapes, append the compensating `consent.granted` with `cascade_recovery = true`, `granted_by` = the operator, `intent_event_id` (or `intent_event_candidates`), and every other field re-derived from the Consent record and the placement; then populate the maps. A consent that stays un-eventful past `compensation_window` escalates exactly as an unpropagated revocation does. [Register Processing] (binding intended but its event lost — mitigated by audit-first ordering: the binding is added to the map only after the event lands, so a lost event means the binding simply is not registered, a fail-safe-toward-omission that a retry corrects) and [Read Consent History] (records read but access unrecorded — mitigated by recording before returning) need no scan leg. This mirrors Defensible Retention's and Customer Onboarding's treatment of their cross-store atomicity holes. Deployments under GDPR exposure must treat a Revoked-but-unpropagated consent as a hard alerting condition.

- **Two Retention Window instances.** There are two distinct Retention Window concepts, and conflating them is a reference ambiguity. (1) The **consent-record Retention Window instance** named in *Composes* holds one placement per consent record (at [Record Consent]), governing how long the controller is obliged to be able to produce the consent record as demonstrability evidence. (2) The **audit-event Retention Window instance** lives *inside the Audit Trail substrate* and governs the lifetime of the consent audit *events* (`consent.granted`, `consent.revoked`, etc.), configured via `audit_trail_retention_policy`. The two instances are separate and their policies differ — audit events should persist at least as long as the consent record they describe, and often longer for litigation defensibility after the consent-record retention's proof period has elapsed.

- **A consent-record retention never reaches `Purged`.** Consent's Invariant 8 keeps every consent record in the store, and this composition does not qualify that — a composition may not weaken a constituent invariant by paraphrase. Retention Window is equally unqualified from the other direction: its `Purged` is defined as the terminal state of a retention *whose underlying record has been destroyed*, and its *Divergence* edge case treats a `Purged` retention over a still-existing record as the compliance failure. Read together, the two atoms leave exactly one lawful lifecycle for the consent-record retention instance: placed at [Record Consent], `Retained` for the life of the store, `Purge Eligible` turning true when the proof period elapses, and never purged. That is not a wasted placement. It puts the proof period on the record as a records-checkable value, it makes the floor structural rather than procedural (Retention Window Invariant 7 rejects an early `purge` even from a host that wires one), and it is the hook a composition that *does* perform destruction would need. So the wording is deliberate throughout: this composition describes the retention obligation as *elapsed* or the retention as *purge-eligible*, never as purged, and never describes the consent record as removed. Whether a consent record may ever be destroyed after the floor — under storage limitation, or a data-subject erasure request — is a question for a composition that performs destruction ([Resolve a Person's Data Rights](./resolve-a-persons-data-rights.md) is the one that would), and answering it is a touch to Consent, not to this pattern.

- **The composition does not perform downstream cessation.** [Withdraw Consent] records that a withdrawal occurred and enumerates the downstream scopes that relied on the consent; it does **not** delete derived data, halt pipelines, or notify third parties. Those are the downstream processors' obligations, signalled by the `consent.revoked` event's `affected_scopes`. This composition is the system of record for *that* consent was withdrawn and *what* relied on it; it is not the orchestrator of *cessation*. A deployment that needs guaranteed cessation wires a downstream-action engine (or a Notification Fanout to the named processors) that consumes the propagation event — a composing peer, not a constituent.

- **[Register Processing] does not gate on consent state.** A processing activity may be registered against a Granted consent before processing begins, and the registration neither requires nor checks that consent is currently `granted` — that is the [Processing Permitted] gate's job at processing time. Registering against a consent that is later revoked is exactly the case the propagation is for: the binding is in `consent_to_downstream`, so withdrawal enumerates it. Registering against an already-Revoked consent is permitted (the binding records intent) but pointless — [Processing Permitted] will reject — and a deployment that wants to forbid it wires the check above this composition.

- **Processing systems reading Consent directly.** Processing systems must call [Processing Permitted] and must **not** read Consent's `check` directly. The prohibition is structural, not stylistic: if each processing system reads Consent and applies its own predicate, the gate is re-implemented per system, and the first system that treats `expired` as `granted`, caches a stale result, or forgets the check breaks the Article 6 basis silently and per-system. Centralizing the gate at [Processing Permitted] makes the guarantee exist exactly once. A deployment in which a processing system reads Consent directly is a composition-bypass finding (Invariant 1's deployment obligation).

- **Registration completeness is a deployment obligation.** Nothing in this composition forces a processing activity to be registered before it processes: [Register Processing] records the binding when called, and the gate does not check registration (it checks consent). An unregistered processor that consumes the gate processes lawfully but is **invisible to the propagation** — a withdrawal's `affected_scopes` cannot name it, so it is never on notice through the records. The composition proves what was registered, never that everything was. The deployment therefore owes a second single-surface obligation, paired with the gate obligation: every consent-based processing activity is registered through [Register Processing] before it first processes. An activity found processing on a consent basis with no `processing.registered` binding is the registration-side composition-bypass finding; verifying that none exists requires the deployment's own processing inventory (the externally-clearable check *Whether the registered set is complete*).

- **Expiry does not propagate — the asymmetry is designed, and bounded.** `Expired` ends the lawful basis exactly as revocation does, but no propagation event is recorded for it, and the asymmetry is structural rather than an oversight: revocation is an *act* — an attributed transition at a commit instant, giving the propagation event a moment, an actor, and a transaction to ride; expiry is a stored deadline *lapsing*, derived by `Consent.check` at read time against the clock at Consent's own seam (Consent stores no scheduler and fires no transition at `expires_at` — the atom's own expiry-is-derived discipline). There is no state change to commit a propagation event atomically *with* — an "expiry event" would need a scheduler this composition deliberately does not own and a clock guard its Logic confinement forbids. The lawful basis still ends enforceably: the gate returns [Not Permitted] (`expired`) from the first post-expiry check, so a gate-consuming processor stops at its next check — the freshness of that stop is bounded by the processor's own check cadence, the same residual the single-surface obligation already carries for every gate answer. A deployment needing *affirmative* expiry notice (processors told, not merely refused) wires a scheduled sweep at the calling layer — a composing peer that reads expiring consents (`Consent.read`), joins `consent_to_downstream` for the registered processors, and notifies them (Notification Fanout); its records, not this composition's, then carry the notice trail.

- **Self-service withdrawal and Article 7(3) ease.** GDPR Article 7(3) requires withdrawal be as easy as grant. That ease is a property of the data-subject-facing surface (the unsubscribe link, the preferences toggle) upstream of this composition. This composition's [Withdraw Consent] is permission-gated because it is the *operator-side* recording of a withdrawal; a self-service deployment wires the subject's own withdrawal request to an actor holding `consent:revoke` at the calling layer, so the subject's absolute right to withdraw is honored without granting the subject internal Permissions. This composition does not model the data-subject-facing surface; it records the withdrawal the surface produces.

- **Consent for non-consent lawful bases.** GDPR Article 6 names six lawful bases; this composition governs only consent (6(1)(a)). Processing that rests on legitimate interest, contract necessity, or legal obligation (e.g., Customer Onboarding, which rests on 6(1)(c)) is not gated by this composition and must not be — a customer cannot withdraw the basis for AML (anti-money-laundering)-mandated processing. This composition's gate applies only to consent-based purposes; basis selection is upstream.

- **Preference management beyond consent.** A neighboring concept often bundled with consent management under the label "preference management": communication *preferences* that are not lawful-basis consent (preferred channel, frequency caps, quiet hours) belong to the [Message Preference / Personalization](../atoms/message-preference.md) atom and are governed by Preference-Aware Notification Fanout, not this composition. This composition governs *consent as a lawful basis*; where a deployment's "preferences" are in fact consent (opt-in to a marketing channel), they are this composition's; where they are delivery-shaping under an existing basis, they are Preference-Aware Notification Fanout's. The boundary is whether the toggle is the *lawful basis* for processing or the *shape* of processing already lawful.

- **Concurrency.** Concurrent state-changing actions for the same `consent_id` (e.g., two [Withdraw Consent] calls, or a [Register Processing] racing a [Withdraw Consent]) resolve under the host environment's serialization guarantees; Consent's named edge case on concurrent `revoke` governs the constituent layer (the first revoke wins, the second observes `already-revoked`). A [Register Processing] that commits its binding *after* a concurrent [Withdraw Consent] computed its `affected_scopes` (step 4, still step 4 after the intent record was inserted at step 5) would leave that binding out of the propagation event — so **per-`consent_id` serialization of [Register Processing] against [Withdraw Consent] is a hard conformance requirement, not an option**: Generation-acceptance check 2's log-order boundary is sound only under it (an unserialized deployment could land a `processing.registered` event between the step-4 read and the step-7 append — ordered before the `consent.revoked` event in the log yet absent from its `affected_scopes`, the exact state check 2 flags). An implementation that cannot serialize the pair does not conform to this composition's withdrawal surface. [Processing Permitted] is read-only and excluded from the serialization obligation; its single `Consent.check` read is authoritative and any skew fails safe — a stale read can only yield a spurious [Not Permitted], never a false `permitted`.

- **Clock semantics.** `now` — used to stamp `intended_at`, `registered_at` and `revoked_at` on the audit events and on the `consent_to_downstream` binding — is the injected `clock_t` supplied at the composition's single I/O seam (see *Logic confinement*); no action reads a wall clock internally and no signature carries a `now` parameter. The [Processing Permitted] gate reads no clock at all: its point-in-time predicate is `Consent.check`, evaluated against the `now` injected at Consent's own seam. Clock quality — honesty, monotonicity, skew relative to the constituents' own seams — remains a deployment matter. Where withdrawal timestamps have legal force (Article 7(3) disputes turn on *when* consent was withdrawn), the deployment must inject a reading sourced from a trustworthy clock; a composed Trusted Timestamping pattern supplies the verifiable time-anchor and binds Event Log insertion order to wall-time. Absent it, insertion order is authoritative and timestamps are advisory, so a `revoked_at` earlier than a `registered_at` it succeeds in the log is a skew signal in the injected readings, not a reordering of the record. Ids are seam-allocated rather than generated in-transition: this composition introduces no identifier of its own, and `consent_id` / `retention_id` / `event_id` are minted at the constituents' seams. The residual risk under injection is a deployment that injects a dishonest `now`, not an internal race.

#### Record Consent

The composition action that records a data subject's consent for a purpose under an authorized operator's authority: it checks the operator's [Consent Grant] permission, records a `consent.grant_intended` event — the write that verifies the operator's credential, before anything commits — then calls `Consent.grant`, places the consent record under its retention floor, and audits the grant (`consent.granted`, carrying the intent event's id). Returns the `consent_id`.

Kind: Operation

#### Register Processing

The composition action that binds a downstream processing activity ([Processing Scope]) to a consent, making *what relied on this consent* a recorded fact rather than tribal knowledge. It does not check consent state — that is [Processing Permitted]'s job at processing time. Audit-first: the binding enters `consent_to_downstream` only after its `processing.registered` event lands.

Kind: Operation

#### Withdraw Consent

The composition's structural reason to exist: revoke a consent **and propagate the withdrawal** — recording, atomically with the `Consent.revoke`, the complete [Affected Scopes] set (every downstream scope the consent governed) on one tamper-evident `consent.revoked` event. A `consent.withdrawal_intended` event precedes the revoke and is where the revoker's credential is verified, so a terminal transition is never performed on an unverified claim (Invariant 8). Revoke and propagation commit together or not at all (Invariant 3).

Kind: Operation

#### Read Consent History

The permission-gated read surface for compliance dashboards, DSAR workflows, and regulatory reporting. Because access to consent records is itself a regulated act, it records a `consent.history-read` meta-event and refuses to return the records if that meta-event fails to land.

Kind: Operation

#### Processing Permitted

The read-only consent-gates-processing query every processing system consumes before acting on personal data (rather than reading Consent directly). Returns `permitted` exactly when `Consent.check` reports valid consent, else [Not Permitted] naming the actual state. Produces no audit event — it changes no state.

Kind: Operation

#### Affected Scopes

The complete set of downstream processing bindings ([Processing Scope] plus processor) registered against a consent, computed from `consent_to_downstream` at withdrawal time and recorded on the `consent.revoked` propagation event. Its completeness is Invariant 3: the enumeration is exactly what relied on the consent, never silently shrunk, so a withdrawal's downstream impact is a records-alone fact.

## Standards references

- **GDPR Article 6(1)(a)** — consent as a lawful basis for processing. This composition's [Processing Permitted] gate is the structural form of the before-processing basis check; a `permitted` result is the records-alone evidence that a valid consent existed at processing time.
- **GDPR Article 7(1)** — the controller bears the burden of demonstrating consent. This composition's `consent.granted` events plus the retained Consent records (held under the consent-record Retention Window — Invariant 6) are the demonstrability surface; a withdrawn consent is retained as proof the basis once existed.
- **GDPR Article 7(3)** — withdrawal must be as easy as grant, and withdrawal does not affect the lawfulness of prior processing. [Withdraw Consent] is the recording surface; the data-subject-facing ease is upstream (Edge cases — *Self-service withdrawal*); non-retroactivity is inherited from Consent Invariant 9.
- **GDPR Article 17(1)(b)** — right to erasure when consent is withdrawn and no other basis applies. The `consent.revoked` event's `affected_scopes` is the notice that triggers downstream erasure obligations; this composition signals, the downstream processor erases (Edge cases — *The composition does not perform downstream cessation*).
- **GDPR Article 30** — records of processing activities must name purposes and legal bases. This composition's Consent records (`purpose`, `granted_at`) plus the `processing.registered` bindings supply the Article 30 documentation surface for consent-based processing.
- **CCPA / CPRA** (the California Consumer Privacy Act and its amending California Privacy Rights Act) — right to opt out of sale/sharing and opt in for sensitive personal information. This composition's [Record Consent] / [Withdraw Consent] are the opt-in/opt-out recording surfaces; the propagation event names the processors a do-not-sell signal must reach.
- **HIPAA §164.508 (Authorization)** — required Authorization elements (purpose, expiration, right to revoke) map to the Consent record fields; this composition adds the operator-authority gate (Permissions) and the tamper-evident audit the regulated context requires.
- **ePrivacy Directive (Cookie Law)** — consent for non-essential cookies and tracking. A cookie-consent backend records `grant`/`revoke` through this composition; the `consent.revoked` propagation names the tracking systems that must stop.
- **ISO/IEC (International Organization for Standardization / International Electrotechnical Commission) 29184 (Online privacy notices and consent)** — consent record content and lifecycle. This composition's grant/registration/withdrawal records are the lifecycle artifacts the standard describes.

The four constituents carry their own deep standards inheritance — see each constituent's Standards references.
